<h2 id="a-city-that-still-blocked-the-road">A city that still blocked the road</h2>

Tula remained an obstacle because its defenders continued to function. German forces could move around the city, threaten its communications, and reach settlements farther east without extinguishing the Soviet army and industrial community inside it. On 18 November 1941, Heinz Guderian renewed the southern offensive toward Moscow under precisely those conditions. His Second Panzer Army was expected to turn movement beyond Tula into the southern arm of a much larger envelopment. It would have to do so while containing a defended city behind its leading formations and protecting an increasingly exposed eastern flank.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–53: Second Panzer Army's position, 18 November attack and continuing defence of Tula}}

The opening produced a striking local advance. The U.S. Army history reports that one of Guderian's corps covered almost twenty-five miles during the first day. Read alone, that distance suggests the return of the mobile warfare that had carried German forces across enormous stretches of the Soviet Union. Read beside the condition of the army and the tasks still ahead, it becomes more ambiguous. A fast spearhead could reach a new objective before the infantry needed to secure the route, while the surviving Soviet positions continued to threaten the forces passing them. Tactical momentum and operational vulnerability grew together.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–52: first-day advance and strained German formations}}

The [opening study of this phase](/articles/barbarossa-the-final-offensive-1941-11-15) explained why German leaders chose to attack again. The southern sector now shows the consequences of that choice on a more manageable scale. Following the roads east of Tula, then north toward Venev and Kashira, we can see how an attempted encirclement became a competition between advancing columns, defending formations, and the resources each side could bring to threatened places. The city itself never fell. The crucial question was whether Guderian could make its continued resistance irrelevant before Soviet commanders made his exposed advance untenable.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50, 53 and 59–60: southern objectives, Tula defence and Soviet reinforcement}}

<h2 id="the-defence-behind-the-name">The defence behind the name</h2>

The Fiftieth Army had survived the destruction of the Bryansk position in October in a badly damaged condition. Its defence of Tula drew on regular formations and local resources, including the Tula Workers' Regiment, the 156th NKVD Rifle Regiment, and the 108th Tank Division. These components were not interchangeable. Infantry, tanks, artillery, security troops, and workers organized for combat contributed different capabilities. Holding the city required them to function as parts of a defence rather than as a collection of heroic names. A surviving headquarters and a working system for issuing orders were as important as the determination to stand.{{p5-tula-library::Historical narrative: October defence, Fiftieth Army and the local formations that joined it}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 49: Fiftieth Army's incorporation into Western Front}}

On 10 November, the Fiftieth Army had come under Zhukov's Western Front. This brought Tula into the same front command responsible for the principal Moscow approaches and made the southern defence part of a broader allocation of forces. Command responsibility mattered because the danger did not stop at a city boundary. An enemy move east of Tula could threaten roads connecting neighbouring armies, while a Soviet counterattack near the Oka could prevent German forces farther west from helping Guderian. The local battle and the wider front therefore influenced one another even when their soldiers could not see the same battlefield.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–52: command transfer, Oka sector and Forty-Ninth Army counterblows}}

Tula's industrial role made this relationship unusually tangible. Evacuation had removed much of its productive capacity, yet remaining facilities continued to manufacture weapons and repair equipment. A factory could be part of national evacuation policy and still leave behind workers and machinery performing urgent local tasks. The distinction helps explain how a threatened industrial city could support its own defence while losing much of the economic activity that had defined it before the war. Production continued under military necessity and severe disruption, rather than under anything resembling ordinary factory conditions.{{p5-tula-library::Closing historical paragraph: partial industrial evacuation, continued weapons manufacture and armoured-vehicle repair}}

<aside class="history-aside"><p class="eyebrow">A workers' regiment begins with a decision</p><p>The surviving catalogue of Tula's wartime documents identifies a city defence committee resolution of 23 October establishing the Workers' Regiment. It locates the original in the former regional party archive and records its publication in the collection Battle for Tula. The catalogue establishes the decision and its date; it does not by itself establish every man's motive, training, or conduct in battle. The document's existence nevertheless restores an institutional step often lost in commemorative accounts. Workers had to be organized into a formation before their participation could become a military capability.{{p5-tula-regiment-order::Catalogue identity and archival reference for the 23 October 1941 resolution; full text not accessed}}</p></aside>

The people required for those tasks belonged to the same endangered community. Mobilizing a worker for combat, retaining a specialist at a machine, and evacuating a family were competing uses of limited time and transport. The sources do not permit a reconstructed conversation for every workshop or household, but the competing demands themselves are clear. Tula's resistance was sustained by the relationship between civilians and military institutions, with all the disruption that relationship entailed. Treating the city's inhabitants merely as an audience watching Guderian approach misses their place in the operation.{{p5-tula-regiment-order::City defence committee resolution: documented organization of civilian workers for combat}}{{p5-tula-library::Continued defence industry during evacuation}}

<h2 id="the-bypass-opens">The bypass opens</h2>

Guderian's postwar study of flank defence gives a useful account of the routes assigned to his formations, although it must be read as a commander's explanation of his own operation. His army's eastern wing moved toward places such as Yepifan and Mikhaylov, while other formations worked through the Stalinogorsk area toward Venev. The Seventeenth Panzer Division supplied the force directed toward Kashira and its Oka crossing. Other German troops remained concerned with Tula and the communications toward Aleksin. The pattern was already one of dispersed obligations: advance, protect a flank, contain a defended area, and connect with a neighbouring army.{{p5-guderian-flanks::November section, paragraphs beginning On 18 November and Ahead of this Corps: assigned axes and flank missions}}

The names on that itinerary describe different problems. Stalinogorsk lay in the industrial country east of Tula; Venev offered a route farther north; Kashira stood on the Oka system and mattered for movement toward the capital's southern approaches. Reaching one place did not secure the others. Nor could a force directed toward an Oka crossing assume that the surrounding roads and rear areas were safe. A column's position on a map might look like the beginning of an encirclement while the space behind it still contained organized Soviet troops capable of fighting, withdrawing, or striking its communications.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50, 53 and 57: Kashira and the developing Tula bulge}}{{p5-guderian-flanks::November assignments toward Stalinogorsk, Venev, Kashira and Mikhaylov}}

The first day's movement is therefore best understood as an opening. It exposed parts of the Soviet defence and created opportunities for further German advances. It did not produce a completed ring around Tula, still less a connection with the German northern armies. Those larger results required additional fighting and coordination. The offensive's apparent speed was also uneven: mobile troops could exploit a local breach while exhausted infantry and supply columns followed more slowly. The farther those elements separated, the more the army depended on being able to improvise protection with forces needed somewhere else.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–53: initial southern advance and later persistent Tula obstacle}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–45: German vehicle and effective-strength limitations}}

The Soviet response included committing reserves into the threatened area. The 239th Rifle Division was sent toward Stalinogorsk, where its fighting interfered with German progress before it was itself surrounded. Its experience illustrates both the value and the danger of using fresh formations to contain a penetration. A division could delay and damage an attacker without remaining secure. Its subsequent escape from the tightening pocket, acknowledged in Guderian's account, also demonstrates that German encirclements were becoming harder to seal. The fighting continued after a line on a command map appeared to have closed.{{p5-tula-library::Passage on commitment and encirclement of the 239th Rifle Division}}{{p5-guderian-flanks::Late-November account of the 239th Division's breakout through the Twenty-Ninth Motorized Division}}

Guderian also recorded a disturbing incident immediately before the renewed offensive. On 17 November, the 112th Infantry Division encountered fresh Soviet opposition near Uzlovaya and tanks coming from Dedilovo. His account describes panic spreading back toward Bogoroditsk before the neighbouring 167th Infantry Division helped restore the situation. The description is retrospective and comes from the army commander, but it is an unusually explicit admission that German infantry could lose cohesion under pressure. It also gives Soviet action its proper place in the story: German exhaustion became a battlefield disadvantage because an opponent was present to exploit it.{{p5-guderian-flanks::Paragraphs on the 17 November crisis of the 112th Infantry Division and the intervention of the 167th}}

The assignments Guderian listed for 18 November show how much of his force was already occupied with supporting tasks. The Eighteenth Panzer Division was directed toward Yefremov, the Tenth Motorized Division toward Yepifan and Mikhaylov, and the Twenty-Ninth Motorized Division toward the eastern flank. These movements supported the larger operation but did not all point directly toward Moscow. A tally of the army's divisions therefore exaggerates the strength available for any single thrust if it assumes that every formation could be concentrated against the same objective. The army had to move through and hold a broad area while still finding force for its principal operation.{{p5-guderian-flanks::Assignments to XXXXVII Corps formations at the opening of the 18 November offensive}}

The subsequent escape of the 239th Rifle Division had a similarly concrete cost on both sides. Guderian acknowledged that Soviet troops broke through the thin line of the Twenty-Ninth Motorized Division, leaving equipment and artillery behind while saving personnel. The loss of those weapons mattered for the division's future combat effectiveness; surviving men did not immediately constitute a fully equipped replacement formation. For the Germans, however, a pocket that required containment and then failed to destroy its occupants was an expensive result. It demanded combat strength without securing the removal of all the enemy personnel that the operation had intended to eliminate.{{p5-guderian-flanks::Late-November breakout account: abandoned Soviet equipment, surviving men and German losses}}

These examples also caution against assigning consistent morale to an entire army. A Soviet formation could escape a pocket while another fell back under an armoured attack; a German division could suffer panic while a neighbouring formation restored the line. Headquarters had to work with those differences. The southern battle was shaped by particular units retaining or losing their ability to act together, often within a short distance of formations experiencing a very different outcome.{{p5-guderian-flanks::Contrasting accounts of 112th Infantry Division and the 239th Rifle Division}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–53: contrasting local results}}

The projected strike against Soviet communications provides another measure of the operation’s limits. Guderian’s retrospective account records the importance attached to cutting the Ryazan–Kolomna railway, which supported the Soviet position beyond his eastern flank. After reaching Mikhaylov on 27 November, the Tenth Motorized Division sent reconnaissance and demolition parties toward that railway, but they did not reach the intended target. This later development clarifies the ambition behind the movement that began on 18 November. Taking an intermediate town and dispatching a patrol were steps toward disrupting Soviet reinforcement; they were not proof that the railway had been severed. The distinction preserves the difference between an operational order, a reconnaissance effort, and an achieved interdiction.{{p5-guderian-flanks::Account of the revised objectives discussed on 24 November and the Tenth Motorized Division’s unsuccessful railway mission on 27 November}}

<h2 id="a-flank-is-an-obligation">A flank is an obligation</h2>

The central difficulty was the shape of Guderian's advance. His leading troops were being directed northward, but Soviet forces could approach their eastern side from the Ryazan and Kolomna directions. Second Army, farther south, could not simply provide an unbroken protective wall, while Fourth Army had its own difficulties around the Oka. The farther the Second Panzer Army advanced, the longer the vulnerable side of its operation became. Protecting it required troops, reconnaissance, and reserves that were also needed for the main attack. Guderian's later emphasis on this problem was self-interested, but the geography and the continuing Soviet activity made it real.{{p5-guderian-flanks::November section: eastern flank, Second and Fourth Armies, and demands on Second Panzer Army}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–53: army boundaries and separate difficulties}}

A threatened flank is not simply an exposed line on paper. It is a possible route into a supply column, a workshop area, a bridge, or a headquarters. A force defending against it must watch roads and crossings while retaining enough mobility to respond. If it is spread evenly everywhere, it may be too weak at the point actually attacked. If it concentrates, it leaves other approaches open. This was the practical problem behind German complaints about insufficient infantry. The army's mobile formations could create a large salient more quickly than the rest of the army could make that salient secure.{{p5-guderian-flanks::The November operation as a case study in flank protection}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–45: reduced effective strength and transport}}

The Soviet counterblows near Serpukhov made the German dilemma worse. Kluge's Fourth Army had to fight for its own position when Bock wanted it to help complete the larger operation. Some of its regiments were so depleted that their remaining men and junior officers could not reasonably be treated as fresh attacking formations. This was not merely a quarrel among generals about enthusiasm. A neighbouring army that could not move across its own obstacle could not close the gap on another commander's schedule. The southern pincer consequently depended on events that Guderian could influence only indirectly.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 52: Serpukhov counterblows, depleted regiments and Kluge's assessment}}

German success had often depended on accepting flank risks in order to dislocate an opponent before he could respond. In November, however, Soviet resistance was surviving long enough for the consequences of that risk to accumulate. The Fiftieth Army remained at Tula, the Oka remained a significant barrier, and fresh Soviet forces were being directed toward the endangered approaches. Continued advance could therefore enlarge an army's commitments without correspondingly reducing the enemy's capacity to interfere. The operational method depended increasingly on an enemy collapse that had yet to occur.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53, 57 and 59–60: surviving Soviet forces and reserve commitments}}

<h2 id="shelter-as-a-military-resource">Shelter as a military resource</h2>

The harshness of the southern battle reached into civilian homes. On 17 November, the Stavka issued Order No. 0428 directing the destruction of settlements behind German lines to deprive the invaders of shelter. It authorized aircraft, artillery, and small raiding parties, and instructed Soviet forces withdrawing from an area to take the population with them while destroying places the enemy could use. The order's premise was that accommodation had become a resource on which German endurance depended. Its human implications were equally clear: houses and villages could be sacrificed in the pursuit of military advantage.{{p5-stavka0428::Order 0428, 17 November 1941, opening rationale and clauses 1–3}}

An order is evidence of policy and intention, rather than proof that every prescribed destruction occurred. Its wide geographical language should not be converted into an inventory of villages burned around Tula without local evidence. Yet it belongs in this study because it shows what Soviet leaders were prepared to demand of the landscape through which the armies moved. The instruction to evacuate civilians did not make evacuation easy. A family needed transport or the ability to walk, somewhere to go, and provisions for the journey. Destruction removed the possibility of returning to an intact home even when the front later moved away.{{p5-stavka0428::Clauses 1, 3 and 4: geographical scope, population removal and reports of implementation}}

German requisition and occupation placed their own demands on these communities. The Stavka order itself described villagers being driven from shelter by German troops; that assertion should be recognized as the issuing command's account, rather than treated as a complete survey. More broadly, the competition for shelter reveals how inadequate it is to discuss winter only as an encounter between soldiers and temperature. Warmth depended on buildings, fuel, clothing, food, and the power to obtain them. Armies exercised that power over people whose own survival depended on the same things.{{p5-stavka0428::Opening assessment of German billeting and expulsion of inhabitants; attributed as the order's account}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 45: shortages of winter clothing and supply capacity}}

For the soldiers, warmth also affected combat performance. Rest in shelter could help a man recover enough to march, maintain equipment, or stand another watch. A vehicle required fuel and attention to remain usable; the men caring for it needed time and physical capacity. Those relationships explain why exhaustion could compound even while the ground seemed better suited to movement than it had been in October. Hard surfaces removed one obstacle. They did not remove the need to keep an increasingly dispersed army fed, rested, repaired, and supplied.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 51–52: improved ground mobility and mechanical limits}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–45: transport and winter supply deficiencies}}

<h2 id="toward-kashira-and-the-counterstroke">Toward Kashira and the counterstroke</h2>

The advance beyond 18 November developed through several further engagements. German forces pushed through the country east of Tula and toward the approaches to Kashira, while Soviet commanders attempted to contain the penetration. It is important to keep these subsequent events in sequence. The threat to Kashira belongs to the later November fighting; it was not a completed achievement of the opening day. The Seventeenth Panzer Division's northward movement helped form the large bulge around Tula described in the official history, but the city continued to resist inside the threatened arc.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 57: later Seventeenth Panzer Division thrust and surviving Fiftieth Army}}

The Soviet response around Kashira included cavalry, armour, and infantry. In the late-November reinforcement allocation, two cavalry divisions formed the First Guards Cavalry Corps under Pavel Belov, with additional forces directed into the threatened sector. Cavalry in this setting should not be imagined solely as mounted men charging tanks. Its operational value included movement, reconnaissance, and the ability to bring armed men to places where transport was scarce. Together with tanks and infantry, it helped contest the German thrust and threatened the position of a force whose communications were already stretched.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 59: Soviet cavalry and armour response; Belov's corps and reinforcement allocation}}

<aside class="history-aside"><p class="eyebrow">An army still arriving</p><p>The Tenth Army would eventually strengthen the region beyond Guderian's eastern flank, but its movement belonged to the last week of November. Its commander, Filipp Golikov, received marching orders on 24 November. The army had to cover the long journey from Syzran by rail and on foot because it possessed little motor transport. Its appearance on a strategic map therefore represented a process unfolding over days. A headquarters assignment could precede the arrival of all the troops needed to carry it out.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 60: Golikov, 24 November marching order and movement from Syzran}}</p></aside>

This sequence explains why German commanders could experience Soviet strength as a succession of unwelcome surprises. The reinforcements did not have to arrive everywhere at once to frustrate an offensive. A new force at a threatened crossing could prevent an immediate success; another formation farther east could make an exposed flank harder to ignore. German troops then spent additional time and ammunition solving problems outside their original objective. Soviet reinforcement was uneven and costly, but its cumulative effect was to deprive the attacker of the uninterrupted sequence on which his larger design depended.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–60: separate reserve commitments and army assembly}}

<h2 id="what-the-southern-advance-meant">What the southern advance meant</h2>

The southern pincer demonstrates why kilometres alone provide an inadequate measure of an offensive. A formation could travel quickly on one day and still finish the week less able to complete its mission. It might gain ground while leaving a defended city behind, expend fuel without gaining a usable railway, or create a salient that demanded more troops than it released. Guderian's force remained dangerous, and Soviet commanders could not dismiss its progress. Yet the relationship between movement and strategic effect was becoming less favourable to the Germans with each new obligation.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–53 and 59–60: local advance, continuing Tula defence and Soviet response}}

The wider war was also moving on 18 November. Britain opened Operation Crusader in North Africa, adding pressure in another theatre while Anglo-American assistance to the Soviet Union developed. That operation was not an immediate rescue for a particular Soviet battalion near Tula, and it should not be inserted into the local battle as though it were. It did, however, underline the German strategic problem: the eastern campaign was continuing while other commitments remained active. German planning could no longer assume that defeating the Soviet Union would quickly release its resources for a different war.{{p5-stahel-long-road::Chapter 6 public extract: 18 November opening of Crusader and parallel strategic pressures}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–46: German recognition of a continuing eastern campaign}}

Tula's defenders had made that wider failure concrete. By holding the city, sustaining military organization, and preserving useful local capacities, they forced the attackers to keep spending resources on a problem that the plan required them to leave behind. Their achievement was neither effortless nor inevitable. It included dangerous penetrations, encircled Soviet formations, strained communications, and civilians whose lives were subordinated to the demands of survival and defence. The city remained a functioning obstacle because people continued to make it one.{{p5-tula-library::Defence, partial encirclement and continued industrial activity}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 57–60: Tula and the southern reserve response}}

Later attempts to close the ring would continue into December and belong to the [study of the advance's limits](/articles/barbarossa-the-limits-of-the-advance-1941-12-02). Meanwhile, farther south, German forces were approaching a different city whose capture appeared to promise a major strategic gain. The [fall of Rostov](/articles/barbarossa-rostov-falls-1941-11-21) would test another version of the same question: whether the army taking an important place could also secure the conditions necessary to hold it.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 54–55 and 62–65: distinct Rostov sequence and later Tula operations}}
