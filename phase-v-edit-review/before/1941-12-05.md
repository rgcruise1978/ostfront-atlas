<h2 id="the-end-of-an-expectation">The end of an expectation</h2>

On the morning of 5 December 1941, Heinz Guderian still hoped to complete the encirclement of Tula. By evening, the commander of Second Panzer Army was proposing to pull his exposed forces back toward the Don and Shat rivers. The change did not represent a sudden discovery that winter existed. It followed repeated failures to eliminate Tula, Soviet pressure against the army's extended positions, and the declining ability of its troops and vehicles to continue offensive operations. One of the commanders most closely associated with German armoured warfare was now trying to preserve the force that had been sent around Moscow's southern flank.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 63–65: Guderian's assessments and proposed withdrawal on 5 December}}

Farther north, Soviet troops of Ivan Konev's Kalinin Front were beginning counteroffensive operations. The broader attacks associated with the Moscow counteroffensive would develop from 6 December, with different armies entering the fighting at different times. There was no single instant when an entire front turned around. German attacks had continued while Soviet counterattacks were already taking place, and preparations for wider operations had overlapped with the immediate effort to contain dangerous penetrations. The transition was a process experienced unevenly by armies separated by hundreds of kilometres.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 61–67: preparations, Kalinin opening and staggered commitment}}

This is why 5 December serves as the endpoint of this atlas's Barbarossa narrative without being presented as a universally agreed formal termination date. The essential German expectation had failed: the invasion had not destroyed the Soviet state's capacity to organize sustained military resistance. Moscow remained under Soviet control, Leningrad had not surrendered, and Rostov had been recovered by Soviet forces. Yet enormous territories remained occupied, German armies retained substantial fighting power, and millions of people faced a winter under siege, in captivity, or beneath an occupation regime. Strategic failure for the invader did not immediately become safety for the invaded.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 55–68}}{{p5-cmh-crimea::Chapter VI, printed pp. 105–106: continuing German control and Sevastopol resistance}}

The [previous study](/articles/barbarossa-the-limits-of-the-advance-1941-12-02) examined the final attempts to extract a decisive result from depleted formations. This concluding chapter widens the view. The question is how a campaign that had inflicted extraordinary destruction nonetheless failed to produce its intended political and military conclusion. The answer requires connecting the battlefield with transport, institutions, industrial survival, and the lives of people whose labour and suffering sustained the war. None of those explanations can replace the fighting itself; together, they explain why its results diverged from German expectations.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–68}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Manuscript pp. 9–10: evacuation and industrial disruption}}

<h2 id="moscow-a-counteroffensive-in-stages">Moscow: a counteroffensive in stages</h2>

The German position around Moscow had become a collection of exposed advances rather than an effective encirclement. Third Panzer Group, commanded by Georg-Hans Reinhardt, had reached the canal approaches northeast of the capital but could not turn that achievement into a secure advance southward. Erich Hoepner's Fourth Panzer Group was checked on the northwestern approaches. Günther von Kluge's Fourth Army had gone over to the defensive after its latest attempt to break through farther south. Guderian's army remained extended around an undefeated Tula. These formations faced different immediate problems, but all depended on a degree of mutual support that the campaign had failed to produce.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 62–65: German dispositions and termination of attacks}}

Soviet planning aimed to exploit the protruding German wings. Around the northern approaches, movements toward Klin and Solnechnogorsk threatened the roads on which the advanced panzer formations depended. Recovering those junctions could make positions closer to Moscow untenable without requiring the Red Army to attack every strongpoint directly. In the south, pressure from the Kashira and Tula sectors, combined with the arrival of Tenth Army farther east, could force Guderian to shorten his front. The operational purpose was to disrupt the German arrangement of forces and communications, as well as to regain ground.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–67: Soviet counteroffensive planning and axes}}

Georgy Zhukov's Western Front possessed new opportunities, but its forces had not assembled in ideal conditions. Vasily Kuznetsov's First Shock Army had already been committed near the canal. Dmitry Lelyushenko's Thirtieth Army was positioned to threaten the northern side of the German advance. The Twentieth Army was entering the battle on the approaches between them and the established defenders, while Konstantin Rokossovsky's Sixteenth Army had borne prolonged pressure farther west. The existence of these headquarters did not mean that every assigned unit had arrived, completed its preparation, or received the transport needed for a sustained pursuit.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 57–62 and 65–67: command arrangements and reserve readiness}}

The chronology reflects those limitations. Konev's Kalinin Front opened its attacks on 5 December. Western Front's broader counteroffensive developed from the following day, and the main entry of Sixteenth Army came later still. In the southern sector, Filipp Golikov's Tenth Army began operations while much of its strength was still arriving. Soviet formations confronting the German Second Army around the Yelets sector also entered a developing offensive rather than a perfectly synchronized blow. Later maps compress these movements into clear arrows; the men carrying them out encountered incomplete concentration, difficult roads, and resistance that varied sharply between neighbouring sectors.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 64–67}}{{p5-cmh-counteroffensive::Chapter IV, printed p. 70: Sixteenth Army enters the counteroffensive on 7 December}}

There was uncertainty at the highest level as well. Konev had questioned whether his front possessed adequate resources for an offensive, and Aleksandr Vasilevsky visited during the night of 4 December to settle arrangements. The decision to attack was consequently more than the execution of a plan made obvious by overwhelming superiority. Soviet commanders were accepting risks while the Germans were still dangerous. Their opportunity arose partly because the enemy had concentrated its remaining strength in positions that were difficult to sustain, leaving little capacity to respond when pressure developed against several vulnerable points.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 64–66: Konev, Vasilevsky and the opening decisions}}

<h2 id="the-northern-war-beyond-moscow">The northern war beyond Moscow</h2>

Army Group North's position shows why the campaign cannot be understood solely from the approaches to the Soviet capital. Wilhelm von Leeb's forces continued to maintain the blockade of Leningrad while their advance toward Tikhvin had created another exposed commitment. Tikhvin mattered because its railway lay within the transport system supporting the Ladoga approaches. German possession threatened supply arrangements for the besieged city and offered the prospect of extending pressure eastward. Holding that advanced position, however, required troops and supplies that were also needed elsewhere along the army group's broad front.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48 and 56–57: Army Group North and Tikhvin}}

Kirill Meretskov's Fourth Independent Army had been fighting to reverse the German advance during November. Its early efforts had limited reinforcement and did not produce an immediate liberation of the town. By 5 December, regrouping and additional forces enabled Meretskov to exert pressure toward Tikhvin from three sides. This was a change in the local balance, achieved through continued preparation and combat. It did not mean that Tikhvin was already back in Soviet hands on the date of this chapter, or that Leningrad's blockade had been lifted.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 51 and 56–57}}{{p5-cmh-counteroffensive::Chapter IV, printed p. 71: Meretskov's position by 5 December}}

The problem facing Leeb was therefore connected to Moscow without being a subordinate episode in the same battle. He feared that the failure of the German assault on the capital would allow the Soviets to apply more strength in the north. Meanwhile, forces already opposite him were demonstrating that the Tikhvin position could be attacked. German plans for eventual contact with Finnish forces farther east remained aspirations dependent on resources and geography. They should not be mistaken for an accomplished link that had sealed every Soviet route around Ladoga.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 56–57}}{{p5-cmh-counteroffensive::Chapter IV, printed pp. 69–71: intended Finnish connection and Leeb's assessment}}

For Leningrad's civilians, this distinction was fundamental. Military progress outside the city mattered only insofar as it helped keep people supplied and alive. Recovering a transport route would require its restoration and operation before it could translate into reliable food deliveries. Hunger also reduced people's ability to work, queue, collect fuel, or care for relatives. The siege was consuming the capacities on which collective survival depended, and a favourable report from the front could not immediately repair that damage.{{p5-leningrad-diaries::Lena Mukhina, diary entry of 21 November 1941; Yura Ryabinkin, entries of 6–7 November 1941}}

Her account listed 125 grams of bread and 200 grams of sweets, the sweets intended to last ten days. She had already eaten more than the allowance she had set herself. The diary preserves the arithmetic of hunger without turning the writer into a symbol of effortless endurance. Celebration, guilt, affection, and anxiety occupied the same day. Her ration and experience should not be generalized to every resident, but they reveal what the continuation of the siege meant inside one household.{{p5-leningrad-diaries::Lena Mukhina, diary entry dated 21 November 1941, selected text reproduced by Arzamas}}</p></aside>

<h2 id="the-south-after-rostov">The south after Rostov</h2>

The southern theatre had already supplied a clear German reverse. The [Rostov counteroffensive](/articles/barbarossa-rostov-retaken-1941-11-29) forced First Panzer Army away from the lower Don and helped precipitate Gerd von Rundstedt's dismissal. By early December, however, Army Group South had established a defensible position farther west along the Mius and Donets system. Walter von Reichenau, Rundstedt's replacement, had accepted the need for withdrawal once he confronted the operational circumstances. The retreat removed an exposed advance; it did not erase the army group's earlier conquests.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 54–56: Rostov, command changes and withdrawal}}{{p5-cmh-crimea::Chapter VI, printed p. 105: early-December southern front}}

Sixth Army retained Kharkov, Belgorod, and Oboyan on the army group's northern side. Seventeenth Army and First Panzer Army held positions across the western Donbas and toward the Mius. These were territories of industrial, agricultural, and transport importance, with populations living under German occupation. Soviet recovery of Rostov had frustrated an immediate German approach toward the Caucasus and improved the southern strategic position, but it had not recovered the whole Donbas or placed every German formation in retreat. The map remained a record of both Soviet resistance and extensive German territorial power.{{p5-cmh-crimea::Chapter VI, printed p. 105: Sixth, Seventeenth and First Panzer Army positions}}

In Crimea, Erich von Manstein's Eleventh Army confronted another failure to turn an advance into the elimination of organized resistance. Sevastopol remained in Soviet hands. Ivan Petrov's Independent Maritime Army, reinforced by men evacuated from Odessa, had helped prevent the fortress from being overrun in early November. Naval gunfire and supply by sea supported the defence. Under Filipp Oktyabrsky, the Sevastopol Defence Region had strengthened its landward positions during November, while rain delayed the next organized German assault. The city's continued resistance tied down forces and preserved a Soviet base within the peninsula.{{p5-cmh-crimea::Chapter VI, printed pp. 105–106: Petrov, Oktyabrsky, reinforcement and preparations}}

The maritime dimension changes the meaning of an encirclement on land. A fortress connected to the Black Sea Fleet could receive assistance despite enemy control over its landward approaches. Naval movement was vulnerable to attack and weather, but it offered a route that an army map alone might obscure. Conversely, concentrating an army against the fortress left other coastal obligations competing for manpower. In this theatre, as around Moscow, success depended on more than pushing the leading infantry farther forward; it required closing or controlling the communications that sustained the opponent.{{p5-cmh-crimea::Chapter VI, printed pp. 106–107: sea communications, terrain and competing demands}}

These regional differences prevent a misleading picture of simultaneous German collapse. By 5 December, the invasion had failed to achieve its intended decisive result, but the conditions of that failure varied. Near Moscow, renewed offensive operations were ending under growing Soviet pressure. Around Tikhvin, an advanced German position was becoming increasingly precarious. In the south, a major withdrawal had already occurred, while powerful occupation forces continued to hold an extensive front and prepared further operations. A sound conclusion must preserve those differences while explaining the common limits they exposed.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 55–68}}{{p5-cmh-counteroffensive::Chapter IV, printed p. 71}}{{p5-cmh-crimea::Chapter VI, printed pp. 105–107}}

<h2 id="why-the-resources-did-not-match-the-objectives">Why resources no longer matched objectives</h2>

The German army had not simply reached a predetermined geographical limit. Its effective reach contracted as casualties, mechanical wear, supply difficulties, and Soviet resistance accumulated. Every further advance added roads to protect and distances over which supplies had to move. Rail deliveries still had to be transferred to vehicles or horse-drawn transport and carried to units whose positions changed. A shortage near the front could therefore exist even when supplies were available farther back. The decisive question was whether the distribution system could deliver what a formation needed before its next engagement.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–46: transport capacity, winter preparations and serviceability}}

Withdrawal did not instantly solve those problems. Units still needed fuel to move, traction to extract guns, and routes that could carry troops and equipment without becoming blocked. A force depleted during attack might lack the resources required for an orderly change of position. That danger helps explain the German commanders' anxiety as offensive prospects disappeared. Remaining forward exposed them to Soviet attacks; moving backward risked losing equipment that could not be recovered in time. The choice was increasingly between difficult courses, rather than between continued victory and a comfortable defensive alternative.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 63–65: Bock's apprehensions and Guderian's withdrawal decision}}

Soviet survival rested on institutions capable of continuing to raise forces and sustain resistance after enormous losses. Industrial evacuation contributed to that capacity, but its effects must be placed on the correct timescale. Mark Harrison's research emphasizes the disruption involved in moving factories and their workforces, the differences in how quickly enterprises resumed production, and the improvisation required at receiving sites. Removing machinery from the enemy's path preserved a possibility; buildings, power, labour, and supply connections still had to make that possibility productive. The later achievements of Soviet industry should not be projected backward as if they were fully available in November.{{p4-harrison-industry::Manuscript pp. 9–10: evacuation, uneven restart and industrial disruption}}

For the armies defending Moscow, reinforcement likewise depended on converting administrative decisions into physical presence. Golikov's Tenth Army had moved from the Volga region with limited motor transport, partly by rail and partly on foot. Its gradual arrival illustrates both the Soviet state's remaining reach and the cost of exercising it. Men could be allocated to an army before their formation was ready for sustained operations. Soviet endurance was therefore neither an inexhaustible reserve nor a miracle of will. It was an uneven achievement involving mobilization, transport, command, and the commitment of people who frequently arrived underprepared.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–60 and 66–67: Tenth Army movement and commitment}}

<h2 id="judgment-weather-and-human-responsibility">Judgment, weather, and human responsibility</h2>

German intelligence and command judgment compounded the material problem. Bock continued to interpret Soviet reinforcements as transfers from other parts of an already strained front, rather than accepting the scale of the reserves becoming available. Reports that supported the prospect of Soviet collapse retained influence after repeated experience had weakened that expectation. The resulting error was operationally consequential: an enemy assumed to be near the end of its resources could be pressed for one more effort, whereas an enemy capable of mounting new attacks required precautions the Germans increasingly lacked the strength to take.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 61–64: assessments of Soviet reserves and offensive prospects}}

Weather interacted with this misjudgment; it did not absolve it. Frost had initially improved movement over ground previously impeded by mud. More severe cold then damaged readiness, complicated engine starting and maintenance, and increased exposure among inadequately equipped men. Soviet troops also marched, fought, and waited outdoors in those conditions. Their ability to exploit German weaknesses required effort and losses. Explaining the reversal through weather alone obscures the Soviet operations that created pressure and the German decisions that left depleted formations dependent on another rapid success.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 51–53 and 63–67}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–46}}

The suffering of German soldiers can be acknowledged without separating the invading army from the character of its war. Soviet prisoners were dying under a system shaped by racist policy, deliberately inadequate provision, exposure, and shootings. Military authorities participated in that system. The absence of food and shelter for prisoners cannot be explained simply by extending the experience of a cold German infantryman to everyone behind the front. Those held captive lacked the power to change their circumstances and were subjected to policies that treated their survival as expendable.{{ushmm-pow-treatment::Sections Germans Reject Geneva Convention, Policy of Mass Starvation in the East, Epidemics and Mass Shootings}}

Civilian vulnerability also crossed the line between operational decisions and policy. The Soviet order of 17 November to destroy settlements in specified areas behind German lines aimed to deny shelter to the invader. Its terms also required removal of inhabitants during withdrawals. Such an order imposed grave consequences on communities whose homes and means of survival lay within the threatened zones; the existence of an instruction to remove people does not establish that evacuation was practicable or completed everywhere. Soviet defence preserved the possibility of liberation while also drawing civilians into coercive and destructive measures that require their own scrutiny.{{p5-stavka0428::Stavka Order No. 0428, 17 November 1941, especially clauses 1 and 3; translated document}}

An account of endurance must therefore retain the people whose losses do not appear as changes in territorial control. The recapture of a city did not restore murdered relatives. The preservation of a factory did not guarantee decent housing or food for its workers. A successful defensive action could leave villages damaged and families displaced. These consequences were part of the campaign's operation, because armies drew upon labour, transport, shelter, and food from the societies around them. They also remained historical experiences in their own right, beyond whatever military advantage their suffering helped produce.{{p5-winkler-rostov::Printed pp. 70–72: first occupation and civilian testimony}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Manuscript pp. 9–10}}{{p5-stavka0428::Order No. 0428, destruction and removal provisions}}

<h2 id="a-war-that-would-continue">A war that would continue</h2>

Soviet survival also kept an emerging international coalition in the war. Britain was already fighting Germany, while American material support was developing before the United States formally entered the European conflict. Diplomatic records show that this relationship involved practical disputes and delays as well as declarations. On 24 November, American officials discussed shipping commitments, assembly arrangements, and the difficulty of maintaining the promised flow of aid. Those exchanges reveal a coalition being constructed through transport capacity and administrative decisions, rather than one whose eventual resources were already fully deployed on the Moscow front.{{p5-frus813::FRUS 1941, volume I, document 813, 24 November: shipping, protocol commitments and aircraft assembly}}

Neither British assistance nor American commitments should be removed from the explanation of Soviet endurance, but their significance depends on timing. An allocated aircraft had to be shipped, delivered, assembled where necessary, and brought into use. A promise of future equipment could influence confidence and planning without placing a weapon in a soldier's hands that day. The Soviet state and its population were carrying the immediate burden of the land war against the main German forces in the east; the coalition's longer-term potential mattered because the invasion had failed to end that resistance quickly.{{p5-frus813::Document 813: practical distinction between commitments and delivery}}{{p5-frus653::FRUS 1941, volume I, document 653: Soviet assessments and expectations of British assistance}}

What had failed by 5 December was the expectation that successive encirclements and territorial advances would culminate in a Soviet collapse within the campaign's intended horizon. Soviet armies had suffered disasters and continued to make costly mistakes. Nevertheless, new formations appeared, defended cities remained connected to wider systems of support, and counterattacks forced German commands to confront threats they could no longer meet by another uncomplicated advance. Capturing more territory had not removed the opponent's ability to make decisions, move resources, and organize violence on a large scale.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–68: force generation, planning and counteroffensive opening}}

The winter campaign would test how far the Red Army could convert that survival into recovery. Its opening gains, the difficulties of pursuit, and the German effort to stabilize the front belong to the story that follows. They were not accomplished facts on 5 December. At the close of this phase, the achievement was more specific and still immense: the Soviet state remained capable of fighting, and German armies faced a war whose duration and demands exceeded the assumptions that had carried them across the frontier. The people living through that change could not yet know its final outcome. They knew that the fighting would continue, with all the labour, fear, loss, and possibility that continuation contained.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 64–68}}{{p5-cmh-counteroffensive::Chapter IV, printed pp. 69–73: opening developments beyond the phase endpoint}}
