<h2 id="an-offensive-in-instalments">An offensive in instalments</h2>

The last German offensive against Moscow began with an apparent encouragement that could be read in two very different ways. On 15 November 1941, infantry of the Ninth Army attacked south of Kalinin and found Soviet troops withdrawing. Franz Halder, chief of the German Army General Staff, welcomed the departure as an unusual sign of weakness. Yet Georg-Hans Reinhardt, commanding the adjacent Third Panzer Group, offered a less comforting interpretation: the defenders had left screening forces forward while establishing themselves behind a river barrier, and the bridges the attackers wanted had been destroyed. Ground could be gained without destroying the army occupying it. The distinction would haunt the next three weeks.{{p5-stahel-relaunch::Chapter 5, publicly accessible opening extract: XXVII Army Corps on 15 November, Reinhardt's assessment and destroyed bridges}}

This was not the October offensive repeated with fewer kilometres left to travel. Operation Typhoon had opened with concentrated attacks and enormous encirclements. By mid-November, the German command was trying to recover decisive movement from an army whose remaining strength had already been consumed unevenly across a broad front. The new operation began in instalments: a corps of Ninth Army on the 15th, the northern panzer groups on the 16th, and Heinz Guderian's Second Panzer Army south of Tula on the 18th. Those intervals gave the fighting a different rhythm from the great opening blow of October. They also revealed how difficult it had become to assemble a simultaneous effort.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–51: dispositions and staggered 15, 16 and 18 November openings}}

The [preceding study](/articles/barbarossa-the-edge-of-winter-1941-11-14) followed the choices that remained after the October advance. Now those choices became marching orders. Bock needed his northern and southern forces to close around Moscow while the Soviet command was rebuilding the armies that stood between them. Neither side could start afresh. German infantry had lost men and transport across months of fighting; Soviet formations were absorbing replacements into commands still recovering from encirclement. The opening of the offensive would turn on which headquarters could bring usable strength to the next threatened road or river before its opponent did.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–51: Soviet replenishment and German offensive dispositions}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–46: Orsha conference and German resource estimates}}

<h2 id="the-plan-and-its-contradictions">The plan and its contradictions</h2>

Field Marshal Fedor von Bock's Army Group Centre stood in an arc west of Moscow. Adolf Strauss's Ninth Army held the northern sector extending toward Kalinin. Reinhardt's Third Panzer Group occupied the Lama River area west of Klin; Erich Hoepner's Fourth Panzer Group stood farther south, above the main Smolensk–Moscow highway. Günther von Kluge's Fourth Army covered the direct approaches and extended toward the Oka, where it connected with Guderian's Second Panzer Army. The Second Army protected the outer southern flank near Orel and Kursk. Each command had to maintain connections with its neighbours while moving along different roads against different Soviet formations.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–50: German command relationships and geographical deployment}}

Bock's written objectives extended much farther than Moscow's immediate approaches. The Third and Fourth Panzer Groups were to cross the Moscow–Volga Canal and advance toward Rybinsk and Yaroslavl. In the south, Guderian was to reach Kashira; possibilities beyond it included turning toward Moscow or crossing the Oka toward Gorky. Kluge's Fourth Army would help close a nearer ring around the capital. These were several different operations embedded in one plan: a close envelopment, a deeper severing of communications, and an advance beyond the city. Each required infantry, bridging, transport and ammunition that would also be needed by the others.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 50: Bock's deep objectives and Fourth Army's intended role}}

The contradiction was that Bock himself doubted whether his available tanks, men, and supplies could carry the northern forces beyond the canal or the southern forces beyond the Moscow River. He expressed those reservations while leaving the larger orders in force. His subordinates consequently had to reconcile ambitious written missions with increasingly restrictive practical guidance. Third Panzer Group later described the resulting uncertainty. A commander deciding whether to preserve a flank, pursue a retreating enemy, or push toward a distant crossing needed priorities that could survive contact with the actual condition of his troops. Bock's uncertainty made that calculation harder.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 50 and note 12: unchanged orders and Third Panzer Group's complaint}}

The plan also made Kluge's performance crucial. His Fourth Army occupied the central sector that the armoured thrusts were supposed to envelop, but Soviet pressure near Serpukhov forced it to fight for its own position. On 18 November, Kluge would report some regiments reduced to four or five hundred men, with lieutenants commanding them, and consider a withdrawal toward the Protva. Bock wanted this army to help complete an encirclement; its commander was considering how to shorten a threatened front. The problem was present before any final dash to Moscow could begin. The southern armour could advance while the force meant to connect with it remained pinned down.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–52: Fourth Army role, Serpukhov fighting, regimental strengths and proposed Protva withdrawal}}

<h2 id="what-the-staff-already-knew">What the staff already knew</h2>

The shortage of resources was not a discovery made after defeat. At the Orsha staff conference on 13 November, German officers discussed losses, vehicle availability, railway capacity, and the prospect of continued war in 1942. The figures preserved in the U.S. Army's documentary history are estimates reported within the German command: casualties of approximately 686,000 by 1 November, panzer divisions at roughly thirty-five percent of their original tank strength, and a motor fleet in which only about a third of vehicles were fully serviceable. Such aggregate figures cannot describe every battalion, but they show that the senior leadership knew the army had been profoundly diminished.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–45: dated German staff estimates at Orsha}}

German staff officers rated the army's 136 divisions in the east as the equivalent of eighty-three full-strength divisions. That was an assessment of effective strength, not a literal disappearance of fifty-three headquarters. The surviving formations still had to cover the fronts, guard the flanks and execute the missions assigned to their original establishment. Bock could concentrate his remaining mobile troops for another attack, but concentrating them did not restore the infantry elsewhere. The arithmetic helps explain why the army could appear formidable in a list of divisions while commanders at the front were already debating how much more ground their men could hold.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–45: effective divisional strength and vehicle categories}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 51–53: contrasting progress and exhausted Fourth Army regiments}}

Rail transport exposed the problem particularly sharply. Advancing armies depended on lines that had to be converted or restored for German use, while captured Soviet locomotives and wagons were insufficient for the intended traffic. Moving the front east lengthened the journey from railhead to formation. Trucks required fuel to deliver fuel, and damaged vehicles required workshops, parts, recovery equipment, and time. The loss of a vehicle affected far more than its driver: it reduced the supplies that could be carried on the next journey and increased the work required of those still running. An offensive could extend its reach while progressively weakening the system sustaining it.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 45: railway conversion, captured rolling stock and transport shortage}}

<aside class="history-aside"><p class="eyebrow">Winter clothing that existed elsewhere</p><p>The Orsha discussion recorded winter clothing remaining in storage because bringing it forward would displace other supplies. This is a revealing distinction between national provision and battlefield availability. A coat counted in a depot did nothing for a sentry several journeys away. The command was making transport choices among goods that soldiers needed simultaneously: warmth, food, ammunition, and fuel. It could prioritize one consignment, but that decision did not abolish the requirement for the others. The shortage experienced at the front was consequently also a failure of distribution and planning.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 45: winter clothing left in storage because of competing transport requirements}}</p></aside>

Halder's argument for continuing was that the Soviet Union still possessed enough population and industry to restore its forces if allowed time. Stopping appeared to surrender the remaining chance to weaken that recovery. Yet attacking consumed precisely the men and machines Germany would need for the longer struggle Halder now anticipated. The conference reduced some projected objectives but retained the demand to extract further results before winter. This decision helps explain the offensive's persistence. Senior officers could recognize losses accurately and still draw an unsound conclusion about what those losses required them to do next.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–46: Halder's argument, reduced objectives and decision to continue}}

The rolling-stock figures discussed at Orsha make the dependence on conversion concrete. The Germans had captured about five hundred Soviet locomotives and twenty-one thousand railway cars, quantities the staff considered only a fraction of the requirement. Until lines were converted, German equipment could not simply take over the traffic. This created a constraint that capturing another supply depot could not remove: goods needed compatible vehicles and a functioning route before they could become useful at the front. The estimated shortage concerned the occupied eastern network as a whole, rather than a count of engines assigned specifically to the Moscow operation. It nonetheless helps explain why an advance on several widely separated axes imposed demands beyond the railway system’s available capacity.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 45: captured Soviet locomotives and cars, track gauge and estimated requirements}}

<h2 id="the-armies-facing-the-attack">The armies facing the attack</h2>

Georgy Zhukov's Western Front defended the main approaches to Moscow, with Ivan Konev's Kalinin Front to its north and the Southwestern Front farther south. A Soviet front contained several armies, broadly comparable to a German army group. These boundaries became increasingly consequential as Bock's forces struck at the northern and southern ends of the defence. A reinforcement sent to Rokossovsky near Volokolamsk could not also strengthen Tula, while an attack near Kalinin required coordination with forces operating under a different front headquarters. Stalin's high command, the Stavka, had to decide which commanders would receive the reserves and when they could commit them.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–50: Soviet fronts, armies and defensive responsibilities}}

The defence was being reorganized as the attack approached. Zhukov had received the Fiftieth Army on 10 November when the Bryansk Front was dissolved; the Thirtieth Army would be transferred from Kalinin Front a week later. These changes brought the threatened northern and southern flanks under a more unified command, but command transfers did not automatically repair damaged communications or exhausted units. Konstantin Rokossovsky's Sixteenth Army and the neighbouring Thirtieth Army faced the developing northern threat. At Tula, the Fiftieth Army held a position whose survival constrained Guderian's freedom to move north.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–50: 10 and 17 November command transfers; pp. 57–60: Sixteenth and Thirtieth Armies}}

Replenishment during the pause had given Zhukov more than a change of orders. Soviet official figures, reproduced in the U.S. Army history, credited the Western Front with about 100,000 men, 300 tanks and 2,000 guns during the first half of November. These are aggregate reinforcement claims, not a count of equally trained soldiers and serviceable weapons assembled for one attack. They nevertheless indicate the scale of the effort to restore a defence after October's losses. The troops arrived into armies with different shortages and immediate dangers. Training, equipment, transport and the time available to integrate replacements all limited what the newly replenished formations could attempt.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–49: reinforcement, reserve formation and uneven readiness}}

Stalin also demanded counterattacks. On 14 November, the Forty-Ninth Army struck Kluge's right flank near Serpukhov with cavalry, infantry and armour. Zhukov later recalled opposing such blows because he expected a German offensive shortly; his memoir explains his own position and deserves that attribution. The fighting itself is less ambiguous. Renewed Soviet attacks near Serpukhov obliged Kluge to retain forces and consider yielding ground just as Bock needed him to advance. A counterstroke that failed to destroy a German army could still disrupt its timetable and keep badly needed infantry away from a neighbouring thrust.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 50, notes 13–14; p. 52: Serpukhov counterblows and Kluge's response}}

The Soviet command also sought relief through operations outside the Moscow sector. The Kalinin and Southwestern fronts were to hold the outer flanks of Army Group Centre, limiting Bock's ability to transfer formations inward. Farther away, preparations at Rostov and Tikhvin aimed to force German commanders to attend to threats in their own sectors. Such operations could influence Moscow even when no unit moved directly from one battlefield to another: a reserve committed to restoring a threatened southern position could no longer be offered for a different purpose. The actual effects would depend on the attacks' success, but the intention was to make Germany sustain several urgent battles simultaneously.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–51: Stavka assignments to Kalinin, Southwestern, Southern and Leningrad commands}}

Those plans also reveal Soviet limits. Orders to attack did not ensure that sufficient trained troops, armour, or ammunition were already assembled. At Tikhvin, Kirill Meretskov was rebuilding a damaged army; at Rostov, Semyon Timoshenko was strengthening the southern forces before their operation. Their progress belongs to subsequent studies, but the preparation matters here because Moscow's defenders were part of a wider strategic effort. Zhukov could demand reinforcements without being the only commander who needed them. Stalin and the Stavka had to decide which immediate dangers to absorb and which opportunities might justify spending resources elsewhere.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–51: southern reinforcement and Meretskov's limited additions before the 19 November attack}}

The urgency reached Stalin directly on 19 November. According to the command accounts used by the U.S. Army history, he asked Zhukov whether Moscow could be held. Zhukov requested two additional armies and two hundred tanks. Stalin promised the armies toward the end of the month but said there were no tanks to spare. Even at the centre of Soviet power, a promise of reinforcement carried a waiting period. Zhukov had to keep the defence intact through days in which the German armour was already advancing; Stalin had to preserve enough of the reserve to intervene before the crisis became irretrievable.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 51: reported Stalin–Zhukov exchange on 19 November and promised reserve armies}}

<h2 id="movement-and-resistance">Movement and resistance</h2>

The Ninth Army's advance on 15 November offered a narrow opening, not a general collapse. On the next day, the Third and Fourth Panzer Groups entered the offensive. The clash east of Volokolamsk was especially damaging to Soviet plans: a reinforced counterattack by Sixteenth Army's right flank met the German attack coming the other way. The Soviet force was driven back. The episode illustrates how intentions could collide before either side completed its preparations. An order to attack did not guarantee that a formation would choose the time, location, or circumstances of its first serious engagement.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–51: northern attacks and Sixteenth Army counterblow}}

The Lama River sector shows why even the northern advance cannot be narrated at one speed. Stahel's examination of the opening records describes the Seventh Panzer Division making slow progress on 16 November while the accompanying Fourteenth Motorized Infantry Division secured bridgeheads. That contrast is more informative than a broad arrow labelled German advance. Units operating within the same larger movement faced different crossings, defenders, and immediate tasks. A bridgehead created an opportunity, but other formations still had to reach it, cross, and deploy on the far side.{{p5-stahel-relaunch::Chapter 5 public opening extract: LVI Corps, Seventh Panzer and Fourteenth Motorized Infantry on 16 November}}

Freezing ground initially helped the attackers by hardening surfaces that had trapped vehicles in autumn mud. German armour could again leave roads in places where the terrain permitted. Yet this improvement came with shorter days, snow, reduced visibility, and new mechanical and physiological burdens. Aircraft could not provide the same support whenever cloud or poor visibility intervened. Soldiers still had to dig, march, maintain weapons, and keep watch. Weather changed the balance of tasks; it did not supply the missing infantry or restore vehicles already worn out during the earlier campaign.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 51–52: frozen ground, air support and German expectations of winter}}

The southern offensive would begin on 18 November, and one German corps was reported to have covered nearly twenty-five miles that day. That figure belongs to a particular formation and interval. It cannot be treated as the pace of the whole army group, still less as a rate that could be projected all the way to Moscow. At the same time, Kluge was struggling with renewed Soviet attacks near Serpukhov and reporting severely weakened regiments. Local speed and wider exhaustion existed together. The German problem was converting the former into an operation that could survive the latter.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–52: southern first-day advance and Fourth Army's weakened regiments}}

<h2 id="the-people-inside-the-war">The people inside the war</h2>

The defence of Moscow rested on work that operational summaries tend to compress into a phrase about reinforcements. Workers from the city and surrounding districts had been drawn into militia and rifle formations. Their civilian occupations did not disappear when they received military assignments: the population supplying defenders also had to keep transport, workshops, food distribution, and essential services functioning. The official military history records the formation of these units; its account also makes clear that experience and readiness varied. Mobilization enlarged the pool of people available to the army while creating new burdens of training and support.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48–49: militia, rifle formations and reinforcement}}

Industrial evacuation made the strain on that civilian effort particularly severe. Economic historian Mark Harrison describes machinery arriving at destinations where workshops, power or accommodation were not ready. Some plants resumed work quickly; others remained unproductive well into the following year. Moving a machine east therefore saved an asset without necessarily producing a weapon that could defend Moscow in November. Workers and their families had to endure the same displacement while restoring production. The state's ability to replenish Zhukov's armies depended both on establishments still functioning and on the difficult reconstruction of those that had been moved.{{p4-harrison-industry::Printed pp. 9–10: industrial evacuation, delays restarting production and conditions at receiving locations}}

For Soviet prisoners already in German hands, the approaching winter intensified a catastrophe driven by policy. German authorities subjected prisoners to inadequate food and shelter, forced marches, shootings, and conditions in which disease spread. Their mass mortality cannot be explained as an unfortunate consequence of the same logistical difficulties affecting German soldiers. Decisions about whose lives would be sustained were central to this war. Recognizing an exhausted German infantryman's suffering must therefore remain compatible with explaining the destructive purposes and practices of the army in which he served.{{ushmm-pow-treatment::Sections on starvation, shelter, forced marches and shootings in 1941; later cumulative totals are not assigned to 15 November}}

<h2 id="time-in-moscow-and-washington">Time in Moscow and Washington</h2>

On the same day that German infantry began moving south of Kalinin, an American memorandum recorded anxiety about a very different part of the struggle. W. Averell Harriman was concerned that a State Department communication on petroleum refining equipment and American technicians would make Soviet leaders doubt American sincerity. His secretary conveyed the concern to Edward Page, who recorded a dispute over consultation and responsibility now that aid was being handled through Lend-Lease. This was an internal American disagreement about how to fulfil a strategic commitment, not evidence that a particular cargo had reached the Soviet front.{{p5-frus810::FRUS 1941, volume I, document 810, memorandum by Edward Page, 15 November 1941, printed pp. 860–861}}

Harriman's concern connected a technical disagreement to a political danger. The Soviet leadership had accepted a promise of sustained American assistance, and delays or contradictory messages could make that promise appear unreliable. Yet Roosevelt's financing commitment of 30 October could not itself provide the ships, machinery, technicians and administrative agreement required to fulfil it. Washington was building a relationship whose larger material effects lay ahead. Moscow's immediate defence still depended on what could be brought into action during the next few days, while its survival would keep open the prospect of a much longer coalition war.{{p5-frus810::Document 810: Harriman's concern about confidence and consultation with Lend-Lease authorities}}{{p4-frus799::Document 799, 30 October 1941: Roosevelt's financing and delivery commitment, distinguished from arrivals}}

For German commanders, time carried the opposite pressure. Their own staff discussions accepted that Soviet strength could grow again, yet the attempted answer was another advance by forces whose replenishment was already inadequate. The first days offered enough movement to sustain hope and enough resistance to expose its weakness. The offensive had not yet failed in every sector, and Soviet commanders could not safely behave as though it had. But its opening already contained the central problem of the campaign: Germany was trying to obtain a decisive strategic result from advantages that became smaller as they were used.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–46: prospects for 1942 and continued offensive}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–52: opening progress and discussion of the operation's prospects}}

The next instalment would test that problem south of Moscow. Guderian's force could still move rapidly when a local defence gave way, but Tula remained in Soviet hands, and the roads beyond it led into another set of obligations and dangers. The [southern pincer](/articles/barbarossa-the-southern-pincer-1941-11-18) would have to do more than travel around an obstacle. It would have to turn a vulnerable advance into a secure operational result before the defenders could respond.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–53: Second Panzer Army's objectives and continuing defence of Tula}}
