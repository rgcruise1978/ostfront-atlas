<h2 id="the-road-beyond-klin">The road beyond Klin</h2>

Bock had ordered Reinhardt to take Klin and then turn southeast toward Solnechnogorsk. When Klin fell, the route was already crowded by another success: Hoepner's troops had reached Solnechnogorsk from the west. Bock redirected Reinhardt due east, with instructions to cover Hoepner's northern flank as he went. By 24 November 1941, the German advance had gained two important junctions but was still changing shape. The Soviet defence was under severe pressure, yet the attackers' next task remained unsettled. Reinhardt was expected to protect a neighbouring advance while pushing toward the Moscow–Volga Canal with the same increasingly depleted force.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 52–53: orders of 20 November, Klin on 23 November and redirection of Third Panzer Group}}

Army Group Centre's northern wing consisted principally of Georg-Hans Reinhardt's Third Panzer Group and Erich Hoepner's Fourth Panzer Group, with Adolf Strauss's Ninth Army farther north and Günther von Kluge's Fourth Army to the south. These relationships shaped the movement of individual divisions. Reinhardt had to protect Hoepner's exposed northern side while continuing east; Hoepner had to press toward Moscow while maintaining contact with Kluge's much slower infantry. Success in one sector could consequently create a longer flank or a wider gap in another. An army group's frontage was held together by soldiers and communications, not by the continuous line that a staff officer could draw across a map.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–50 and 52–53: army dispositions and interdependence}}

The [opening study of this phase](/articles/barbarossa-the-final-offensive-1941-11-15) followed the decision to attack. Here the roads beyond Klin bring that decision into contact with a defence that was retreating but still being rebuilt. Rokossovsky and Lelyushenko had to keep their armies fighting while Zhukov sought forces to fill the opening between them. The German commanders needed to exploit that opening before the reserves arrived. The struggle at the canal, examined in the [following chapter](/articles/barbarossa-at-the-moscow-volga-canal-1941-11-28), grew out of this race between a dangerous penetration and the effort to restore a continuous defence.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–53 and 57–60}}

<h2 id="a-plan-revised-on-the-march">A plan revised on the march</h2>

Fedor von Bock had moved his command attention toward the northern wing on 20 November, using his special train as a headquarters. The move reflected his determination to obtain a decision from the formations still making progress. He ordered Fourth Panzer Group eastward south of Klin, strengthening the pressure beside Fourth Army, while Third Panzer Group was to take Klin and then move southeast along the road and railway toward Solnechnogorsk. In principle, these changes would bring the two mobile commands into a closer relationship and increase the pressure on the capital's northern approaches.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 52–53: Bock's move and revised orders}}

The plan changed again when Klin fell. Fourth Panzer Group's left-hand formations were already at Solnechnogorsk, so Reinhardt's proposed turn toward Moscow no longer fitted neatly into Bock's arrangement. Bock ordered him to cover Hoepner's flank while also continuing east as far as possible. The two tasks were compatible only while enough strength remained to perform both. Every additional mile east enlarged the distance over which Third Panzer Group had to protect itself and its neighbour. A command hoping to exploit an enemy withdrawal was simultaneously assuming a growing defensive responsibility.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 53: orders after the capture of Klin}}

Reinhardt's altered direction also divided the immediate purpose of the northern armour. Hoepner was pressing toward the capital; Reinhardt was being sent farther east to protect his side. Bock still hoped that these movements would support one another, but a detour or prolonged fight in one sector could leave the other exposed. His earlier doubts about crossing beyond the canal had not produced a clean replacement for the more ambitious orders. The northern commanders were consequently being asked to interpret a succession of instructions against positions that changed daily. The junctions they captured gave them more choices of route without supplying more troops to use them.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 50: original deep objectives and Third Panzer Group's complaint about unclear missions}}

Klin mattered within this process because of the routes passing through it. Its capture opened the road and railway axis toward Solnechnogorsk and gave the Germans a position from which to continue toward the canal. It also placed Soviet withdrawal routes under pressure and complicated contact between neighbouring Soviet armies. The military value lay in what could move through the junction and what could be denied to the opponent. Taking it did not remove the need to clear roads, repair damage, organize traffic, and protect the approaches against forces still operating nearby.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 52–53 and 57: routes, German advance and Soviet army separation}}

Solnechnogorsk was a related but separate gain. It lay farther along the northwestern approach to Moscow, and German arrival there increased the threat to Konstantin Rokossovsky's Sixteenth Army. Treating Klin and Solnechnogorsk as one simultaneous victory conceals the interaction between the panzer groups. Bock was changing Reinhardt's direction partly because Hoepner's troops had already reached the area toward which Reinhardt had been told to move. The record shows overlapping advances and revised orders, rather than the smooth execution of a fixed timetable.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 53: Fourth Panzer Group already at Solnechnogorsk when Klin fell}}

<h2 id="keeping-the-soviet-armies-connected">Keeping the Soviet armies connected</h2>

The Soviet problem was not simply to occupy another defensive line. The German advance had disrupted the relationship between the Thirtieth Army in the north and the Sixteenth Army farther south. Dmitry Lelyushenko, who took command of the Thirtieth Army on 18 November, inherited a force being pressed toward the space bounded by the Volga and the canal. Rokossovsky's right flank was meanwhile extending toward the north as his army attempted to preserve the approaches to Moscow. Their movements could leave a gap between forces that remained individually capable of fighting.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 57–59: Lelyushenko's appointment, Thirtieth Army's position and Sixteenth Army's stretched flank}}

Command arrangements were adjusted as the crisis developed. The Thirtieth Army had been transferred from Kalinin Front to Zhukov's Western Front on 17 November. This placed the threatened armies within one front's responsibilities, but a change of subordination could not instantly repair their communications or replace their losses. It could help Zhukov allocate reinforcements and direct the defence across the seam. The effectiveness of that decision still depended on orders reaching formations whose positions and needs were changing under attack.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 49: transfer of Thirtieth Army; pp. 57–60: subsequent deployments}}

The danger was clearest on the map north of Moscow. By 27 November, the U.S. Army history describes an opening approximately twenty-seven miles wide between Dmitrov, on the canal, and Krasnaya Polyana. Lelyushenko's Thirtieth Army had been pushed toward the angle between the Volga and the canal; Rokossovsky had to stretch Sixteenth Army's right flank eastward to cover the capital. These were consequences still developing after the 24th. They explain why the loss of Klin was so serious: an army could remain organized and capable of resistance while no longer standing across the enemy's most dangerous route.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 57: 27 November gap between Dmitrov and Krasnaya Polyana and positions of Thirtieth and Sixteenth Armies}}

The first Soviet counterblows had not solved the problem. Sixteenth Army's attack at the opening of the renewed German offensive had collided with Fourth Panzer Group's advance and failed. Forces committed to such an attack could not simultaneously serve as an unspent reserve against a breakthrough elsewhere. That history helps explain the urgency with which Zhukov sought new formations later in November. The Red Army was learning and adapting while still paying for decisions that had weakened its ability to absorb the next blow.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 50–51: Sixteenth Army's failed counterblow and Zhukov's request for additional armies}}

Zhukov's late-November reinforcements included nine rifle divisions, two cavalry divisions, eight rifle brigades, six tank brigades and ten independent tank battalions, according to the Soviet figures reproduced in the U.S. Army history. They were distributed across several emergencies. Three rifle divisions went to Thirtieth Army; cavalry, a rifle division and armour were allocated toward Kashira; other units reinforced Sixteenth, Fifth and Fiftieth armies or remained in front reserve. This distribution shows how the northern and southern battles competed for help. The total was substantial, but it never represented a single fresh army waiting intact behind Rokossovsky on 24 November.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–60 and note 35: reinforcement categories and variable tank strengths}}

Nor should formations assembling beyond Moscow be placed on the battlefield prematurely. The First Shock Army was taking shape around Dmitrov, the Twentieth Army was being organized for the threatened northern approaches, and the Tenth Army was preparing to move toward the Oka sector. These were stages in a process, not proof that a complete reserve force stood ready on 24 November. Soviet strength would become operationally significant through arrival, deployment, and commitment over the following days. The German advance was dangerous partly because it was competing with that timetable.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–60: formation and assembly of First Shock, Twentieth and Tenth armies}}

<h2 id="istra-and-the-cost-of-delay">Istra and the cost of delay</h2>

Farther south, the fighting around Istra illustrates why the northern operation cannot be reduced to the progress of its leading armour. The town, river, surrounding settlements, and approaches along the Volokolamsk road formed a different tactical setting from the route beyond Klin. Afanasy Beloborodov's Seventy-Eighth Rifle Division was among the formations contesting this ground. Its resistance required the Germans to fight for passages through the defended area while the wider pincer continued to develop farther north. The Soviet defence was therefore uneven without being uniformly broken.{{p5-istra-museum::Sections 4–5: fighting around Istra and the Seventy-Eighth Rifle Division}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 53: Fourth Panzer Group's increasingly resistant front}}

Istra's chronology deserves particular care. The New Jerusalem Museum's account describes a Soviet observation post moving to the outskirts on the morning of 27 November and the final abandonment of that line a day later. It also records the Seventy-Eighth Division's redesignation as the Ninth Guards Rifle Division on 26 November. These local details place the final stages of the fighting after this chapter's anchor date. They do not support a simplified statement that Klin, Solnechnogorsk, and Istra all fell on 24 November. The chapter follows the developing operation while keeping those later events in their proper sequence.{{p5-istra-museum::Section 5: 26 November redesignation and 27–28 November withdrawal sequence}}

Beloborodov's division also connects the fighting at Istra to the Soviet command's effort to recognize formations that had held together. Its change of designation was announced in Pravda on 27 November, in a notice dated the previous day. The same announcement listed Guards distinctions for the cavalry corps commanded by Pavel Belov and Lev Dovator. These public awards served the state’s wartime presentation of resistance, but they also establish contemporary unit identities. On the chapter's date Beloborodov still commanded the Seventy-Eighth; in the final November fighting it appears as Ninth Guards. Following the change keeps one formation's continuing battle from looking like the arrival of another.{{p5-pravda-guards::Pravda, 27 November 1941, p. 2: Defence Commissariat announcement dated 26 November, including the 78th Rifle Division and cavalry corps redesignations}}

The consequences appeared in Hoepner's deployment. By 28 November, his Second Panzer Division was stalled west of Krasnaya Polyana, while the Eleventh, Fifth, and Tenth Panzer divisions and SS Das Reich extended along a front to its south. The U.S. Army history describes these formations struggling against mines and strongly defended fieldworks. Their presence on an order of battle could suggest a formidable concentration. Their actual arrangement required them to fight across a broad frontage while keeping contact with Fourth Army. The available armour was being used to force local passages rather than exploiting a general collapse.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 53: positions and obstacles on 28 November; later consequence of the 24 November advance}}

Hoepner's armour was consequently being drawn into successive fights for defended passages. Mines and fieldworks required his formations to deploy across the approaches while Kluge's slow movement on their right compelled them to maintain a broad connection. Reinhardt's progress farther north did not remove these obstacles. The Second Panzer Division could approach Krasnaya Polyana while other divisions remained engaged behind or beside it, leaving the leading force dependent on developments outside its own sector. Soviet troops resisting at Istra and on the neighbouring approaches were buying time in the most practical sense: the panzer group was still fighting there when new Soviet formations began entering the northern gap.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 59–60: German delay and Soviet reserve deployment}}

The next reserve headquarters was already taking shape at Dmitrov. Vasily Kuznetsov took command of First Shock Army on 23 November, but the U.S. Army history reports that he initially had only a rifle brigade at hand. The title implied an unusually powerful attacking force; the immediate reality was a command receiving units during an emergency. Its later accumulation of brigades, battalions, artillery and rocket launchers would give it more substance. For the moment, the advancing Germans and the forming Soviet army were approaching the same canal from opposite sides. The time consumed on the roads west of it helped determine what Kuznetsov would have available when they met.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–60: Kuznetsov’s arrival on 23 November and the successive assembly of First Shock Army}}

<h2 id="what-the-intelligence-picture-missed">What the intelligence picture missed</h2>

The German interpretation of Soviet withdrawals remained dangerously optimistic. David Stahel's account identifies a 22 November assessment by Eberhard Kinzel's Foreign Armies East organization that treated transfers from quieter sectors as evidence that the Soviet command had few reserves beyond those available from the Far East. In reality, additional armies were being assembled in the Soviet interior. The error concerned the relationship between visible battlefield losses and the capacity to generate further organized forces. Severe Soviet losses were real; the inference that they had exhausted Soviet possibilities was not.{{p5-stahel-frozen::Public opening extract: Kinzel's 22 November assessment and reserve armies forming in the Volga region}}

There were also more immediate ambiguities. Third Panzer Group's intelligence officers noticed that retreating Soviet troops were leaving some villages unburned. They considered two explanations: declining morale or an expectation of returning. Neither could be established merely from the condition of the buildings. Yet the interpretation mattered. If an intact village indicated collapse, continuing the pursuit seemed promising; if it indicated confidence in a counterattack, the same observation warned of danger. Intelligence involved choosing among explanations, and the German command's expectations influenced which explanation seemed plausible.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 53 and note 23: intelligence assessment of unburned villages}}

An interrogation report provided a more specific warning. A Soviet NKVD lieutenant who deserted to the Seventh Panzer Division described preparations involving fresh troops near the Germans' open northern flank and an attack toward Klin. According to the U.S. Army history, the report reached panzer-group headquarters only in the second week of December. It survives in the account through a later German intelligence document, so it should not be transformed into an exact transcript of what every headquarters knew in November. It does show how information obtained near the front could fail to influence decisions while it was still useful.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 53 and note 24: deserter report and delayed transmission}}

Bock's decisions were therefore being made with an incomplete picture of the forces beyond the retreat. Kinzel's estimate encouraged the belief that Soviet reserves were nearly spent; the report obtained by Seventh Panzer Division suggested an attack against Klin but failed to reach the relevant headquarters in time. Meanwhile, Bock continued directing Reinhardt eastward while requiring him to cover Hoepner. Intelligence failure here was consequential because it supported the existing plan at the moment when the army was becoming more exposed. It left the German command expecting the defence to weaken as Soviet headquarters were preparing to reinforce precisely the ground the Germans were approaching.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 61–64: intelligence failures, scarce reserves and difficulties of changing posture}}

<h2 id="the-inhabited-landscape">The inhabited landscape</h2>

The towns through which these armies moved were places of work, memory, and family life. Klin's Tchaikovsky Museum had begun protecting its collections months before German troops arrived. The museum's exhibition records the departure of two freight wagons for Votkinsk on 24 August, carrying manuscripts, the composer's library, piano, and other possessions under the supervision of his nephew Yuri Davydov. This was one small part of the wider evacuation, but it illustrates the practical use of warning time. Objects survived because people identified them, packed them, found transport, and accompanied them east.{{p5-klin-museum::Opening exhibition account: August evacuation and Yuri Davydov}}

<aside class="history-aside"><p class="eyebrow">The employees who remained</p><p>The museum names the workers left at the house during the occupation: Matryona Vorobyova, Darya and Nikolai Spirov, and Abram Shapshal. Their duties included cleaning, watching the premises, and caring for what remained. Naming them restores individuals usually absent from an operational account of Klin. The institution's retrospective record cannot reproduce everything they experienced or establish their private thoughts. It does establish that protecting the surviving collection depended on ordinary employees whose workplace had become part of an occupied town.{{p5-klin-museum::Named staff retained at the museum during occupation}}</p></aside>

The same military pressure had consequences for civilians whose property possessed no recognized cultural status. A household could not evacuate on the scale of a museum or a factory, and a place on an eastbound route did not guarantee transport. The northern fighting endangered homes while Soviet policy also authorized the destruction of shelter behind German lines. The [Tula study](/articles/barbarossa-the-southern-pincer-1941-11-18) examines the 17 November destruction order in detail. Here its relevance is the collision between military attempts to deny accommodation to the enemy and civilians' dependence on the buildings that might be destroyed. An order records intended action; it does not prove the fate of every village.{{p5-stavka0428::Order 0428, 17 November: shelter destruction and population removal provisions}}

German soldiers also depended on those buildings as the cold intensified. Accommodation, food, and fuel could become objects of coercive requisition within an occupied community. The command's attention to whether villages remained intact was thus connected to its own ability to sustain troops. This relationship helps explain why the civilian landscape belongs inside the operational narrative. Houses were shelter for residents before they became potential billets; roads linked livelihoods before they carried military traffic. The invasion transformed their use through force, and civilian losses cannot be reduced to the inconvenience those losses caused the armies.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 51–53: cold, villages and military assessments}}{{p5-stavka0428::Order 0428: military importance assigned to village shelter}}

At Istra, the New Jerusalem Museum’s evacuation record shows how uncertain access to transport could be. Forty packed cases were waiting by 11 October, but the five vehicles required to move them had not materialized. The museum account describes the eventual removal of part of the collection in early November using two lorries obtained through the efforts of the Kozhukov siblings. Packing and dispatch were separate achievements. This was a cultural institution rather than a fighting unit, yet its experience exposes the same competition for vehicles that shaped the military operation. An evacuation decision could preserve little unless someone found usable transport before the approaching front closed the remaining opportunity.{{p5-istra-museum::Section on evacuation: forty cases, 11 October preparations and removal with two vehicles on 4–5 November}}

<h2 id="time-for-reinforcements-time-for-decisions">Time for reinforcements, time for decisions</h2>

While these battles unfolded, Western assistance was still passing through administrative and transport systems. A telegram of 24 November from Secretary of State Cordell Hull to the American ambassador in London reported presidential instructions to provide shipping for Soviet protocol commitments, subject to physical limits. It also discussed arrangements for aircraft assembly. The wording matters because it separates an intention to supply from the ability to deliver equipment in usable form. Ships, unloading, assembly, onward transport, and allocation to units all stood between a political commitment and a weapon at the front.{{p5-frus813::Document 813, 24 November telegram: shipping instructions and aircraft assembly arrangements}}

Hull's telegram joined two problems that were often discussed separately: confidence between governments and the physical movement of supplies. Roosevelt wanted the Soviet protocol commitments met; officials still had to find shipping and make arrangements that turned delivered aircraft into usable machines. This was the work required to make an alliance effective over time. It could not replace Zhukov's immediate task of filling the northern gap. On 24 November the relevant clocks were running at different speeds: a German column might threaten a junction that day, while an aid commitment still required a succession of sea journeys, assembly work and onward movements before reaching a fighting unit.{{p5-frus813::Document 813: preliminary arrangements and difficulties in supply flow}}

By the end of this stage, German commanders had gained important junctions and forced serious Soviet withdrawals. Those achievements deserve recognition as military facts. Their strategic value was diminishing because Soviet resistance continued, new forces were assembling, and the German formations had to divide their remaining strength among advance, connection, and flank protection. The next objective, the Moscow–Volga Canal, would make the problem especially clear. Reaching a barrier and crossing it could still be accomplished locally; converting that crossing into a sustainable operation required resources and coordination that the northern pincer was increasingly unable to supply.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 57–60: canal approach, changing missions and Soviet reserve commitment}}
