<h2 id="the-offensive-that-could-still-hurt">The offensive that could still hurt</h2>

On 2 December 1941, the German offensive could still inflict a dangerous local reverse. The previous day, the 258th Infantry Division had broken through the Soviet line south of the Moscow–Smolensk highway. Northeast of Tula, Guderian's Second Panzer Army was attempting another westward movement to cut off the city. These were real operations, undertaken by formations that retained weapons, organization, and the ability to attack. They were also being conducted within an army group whose commander had just warned that it lacked the strength to complete the encirclement of Moscow. The contradiction defines this stage of the campaign.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 61–63: Bock's warning and attacks of 1–2 December}}

Bock's predicament was that a real success could still invite an unattainable conclusion. The 258th Division had opened a breach, but his army group had no substantial fresh force waiting to turn it into a decisive advance. Hoepner and Reinhardt were already engaged on the northern approaches, while Guderian was again trying to close a ring around Tula. Each command needed help from another command that was itself struggling. German troops retained the ability to attack a position; the army group was losing the ability to connect those attacks into the operation for which they had been ordered.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 62–64: local attacks, limited reserves and exposed positions}}

The [canal study](/articles/barbarossa-at-the-moscow-volga-canal-1941-11-28) followed one such opportunity through to its limits. The [recovery of Rostov](/articles/barbarossa-rostov-retaken-1941-11-29) showed a more visible reversal in the south. Around Moscow, the transition was less tidy. German attacks, Soviet local counterattacks, the arrival of reserves, and preparations for a broader Soviet offensive overlapped. The events of 2 December should be read within that unsettled present, rather than as the opening scene of a winter victory whose full result was already known.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 55–67: distinct southern and central sequences}}

<h2 id="a-breakthrough-without-an-exploitation">A breakthrough without an exploitation</h2>

The 258th Infantry Division's attack mattered because it occurred in Fourth Army's sector, where progress had been too slow to support the northern pincer as Bock desired. The Soviet line south of the principal western highway was penetrated on 1 December. For a short time, that success offered the possibility that the centre of the German operation might move again while the panzer groups continued pressing farther north. Bock's assessments on 2 December reflected the resulting uncertainty: he could still tell commanders that the opponent was close to breaking while warning Halder later that prospects were deteriorating.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 62: 258th Division breakthrough and Bock's conflicting assessments}}

By the afternoon of 3 December, the 258th Division was fighting its way back west out of an encirclement. The breach had exposed the advancing force without producing a corresponding collapse on its flanks. Bock had hoped that success in Fourth Army's sector would help revive the wider attack; instead, the division needed to extricate itself. That reversal gives a precise limit to the achievement of the 1st. The Soviet line had been penetrated, and the danger on 2 December was real, but the German command had failed to create the conditions in which its successful infantry could remain forward and continue.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 62–63: breakthrough and division's withdrawal from encirclement on 3 December}}

The date of that reversal matters. On 2 December, its full outcome was still in the future. Bock's renewed hopes were not simply an invention created after the war; a local success existed on which he could base them. His error lay in the larger inference that another success might produce the collapse missing from the campaign so far. The army group's accumulated weaknesses made it increasingly difficult to exploit an opening before Soviet resistance closed it. Tactical opportunity and strategic possibility were moving farther apart.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 61–63: Bock's warnings, hopes and the attack's outcome}}

Fourth Army's commander, Günther von Kluge, later became a convenient figure in explanations that attributed failure to reluctance or insufficient offensive spirit. David Stahel rejects the idea that Kluge possessed an unused force capable of turning the campaign into victory. In the accessible opening of his chapter on the final approaches, he emphasizes the weakness of Fourth Army's right wing and the exhaustion of the panzer groups. This is a scholarly interpretation, but it accords with the operational record of limited advances, formations reduced by losses, and attacks that could not be sustained.{{p5-stahel-gates::Public opening extract: critique of the Kluge missed-opportunity interpretation}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 52 and 62–64}}

Kluge proposed abandoning Fourth Army's attack on the morning of 3 December because it would not get through to Moscow. Bock preferred to wait another two or three days to see whether Reinhardt's movement from the north could change the position. By that afternoon, the 258th Division was withdrawing from its predicament and Fourth Panzer Group was reporting its offensive strength largely exhausted. Fourth Army went over to the defensive on the 4th. The sequence shows the different judgements operating within the same command: Kluge assessed his immediate attack, while Bock kept looking for another formation whose success might make it worthwhile to persist.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 62–63: Kluge’s proposal on 3 December, Bock’s delay and Fourth Army’s transition to defence on 4 December}}

<h2 id="the-two-pincers-no-longer-converge">The two pincers no longer converge</h2>

In the north, Third Panzer Group's turn south from the canal had not produced a decisive combination with Fourth Panzer Group. Reinhardt's formations remained engaged around Yakhroma and threatened along their extended northern side. Hoepner's troops were struggling around Krasnaya Polyana and farther south, with Soviet positions requiring direct attacks. The operational picture therefore differed from the geometric promise of two mobile formations curving around a capital. They were fighting separate, increasingly costly battles while trying to preserve their connection to neighbouring commands.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53 and 60–64: northern dispositions and continuing resistance}}

The Soviet First Shock Army was no longer merely a prospective reinforcement. Its elements had made contact with the Germans late in November, and on 2 December Kuznetsov received instructions for the Zakharov group to attack toward Dednevo and Fedorovka, with Klin as a longer-term direction. The immediate objectives were local. The group included parts of formations that had served as a rearguard around Klin and were now pinned west of the canal. The order combined relief of an immediate tactical situation with the possibility of a wider advance if the fighting developed favourably.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 60–62: First Shock Army and the 2 December order to the Zakharov group}}

The Zakharov group's orders began with the villages directly confronting it, rather than an immediate march to a distant objective. Its troops had served as a rearguard around Klin and were now constrained west of the canal by Reinhardt's spearhead. Recovering freedom to move was part of preparing the larger counterattack. Kuznetsov therefore had to fight for the ground from which subsequent operations would begin, using formations that had already endured the retreat. The direction toward Klin gave the operation a broader purpose, but Dednevo and Fedorovka represented the immediate battle that had to be fought before that purpose could be pursued.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 61–62: immediate and longer-term Soviet objectives}}

South of Moscow, Guderian's operation remained entangled with Tula. The city had not fallen, and bypassing it had created an extended bulge without eliminating the defenders. The westward hook attempted on 2 December sought to close the problem by cutting off Tula and potentially releasing pressure on Fourth Army's right wing. Its strategic value depended on more than reaching another point on the perimeter. The Germans had to maintain the connection around the city while Soviet forces threatened their eastern side and the routes toward Kashira.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 53, 57–60 and 62: Tula, Kashira, Soviet reinforcements and 2 December attack}}

The Soviet response in the southern sector had become more substantial. Pavel Belov's cavalry grouping, now the First Guards Cavalry Corps, operated with supporting rifle and armoured forces around the Kashira approaches. The Fiftieth Army continued defending Tula, while the Tenth Army was moving toward the Oka sector farther east. These formations were not all available in the same condition or at the same moment. Together, however, they made it harder for Guderian to treat the area beyond his leading units as empty space through which an encirclement could be completed.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–60: Belov's grouping, Fiftieth Army and Tenth Army}}

Guderian's own account follows the southern attack into a particularly revealing result on 3 December. Fourth Panzer Division damaged the Tula–Moscow railway and reached the Serpukhov–Tula road, then stopped with its troops exhausted and fuel expended. Here the Germans had actually reached and disrupted a communication, unlike the failed late-November patrols toward the Ryazan–Kolomna railway. Yet the success still did not close the ring around Tula. It created a narrow projection needing support from troops whose own advance was faltering. The achievement was substantial enough to threaten the defenders and insufficient to remove their ability to resist.{{p5-guderian-flanks::December operation narrative: 3 December railway demolition, Serpukhov–Tula road, exhausted fuel and the continuing failure to close the Tula encirclement}}

<h2 id="strength-on-paper-and-strength-in-the-line">Strength on paper and strength in the line</h2>

The casualty figures illustrate the depth of the German problem, provided their categories remain clear. Stahel gives 743,112 German casualties in the war against the Soviet Union by 26 November, explicitly excluding men removed from duty through sickness. That was a cumulative campaign figure, not the losses of the Moscow battle alone. It cannot be compared directly with a daily strength return or treated as the number of men permanently absent. Its significance is the scale of the losses the replacement system had been required to absorb before the final attacks.{{p5-stahel-wire::Public opening extract: cumulative casualties to 26 November and exclusion of sickness}}

<aside class="history-aside"><p class="eyebrow">Twenty men in a company</p><p>Stahel cites a German non-commissioned officer's letter home of 21 November describing a company reduced to twenty men as early as October. The formation later redistributed personnel by dissolving a battalion in each regiment. This was one man's account, not a strength return for every German company. It nevertheless shows how reorganization could improve the apparent condition of surviving units without bringing fresh soldiers into the division. The men still present inherited the duties of the formations that disappeared.{{p5-stahel-wire::Public opening extract: 21 November letter and redistribution of personnel}}</p></aside>

This distinction affected commanders' choices. A division might retain its headquarters, artillery, and administrative services while possessing far fewer riflemen capable of holding or attacking a frontage. Counting divisions alone could therefore exaggerate the force available for an assault. Moving men between units could restore some coherence but could not replace lost experience or eliminate exhaustion. The requirement to guard roads, hold flanks, and occupy defensive positions continued even when the infantry available for those tasks had contracted.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 52: reduced German regiments and command losses}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 44–45: effective strength compared with nominal formations}}

North of Tula, this weakness took the form of troops and vehicles stranded near an objective they had reached but could not exploit. Guderian records Soviet forces on both sides of the German wedge toward the Serpukhov road on 4 December. Third Panzer Division fought east of Tula with little progress, while Forty-Third Corps still had to close the gap from the west. Fourth Panzer Division's position could not be secured merely by keeping its remaining tanks there. It depended on infantry and neighbouring formations reaching the threatened ground before Soviet attacks cut the narrow connection back to the army.{{p5-guderian-flanks::Narrative for 4 December: Soviet forces beside the Serpukhov-road wedge, Third Panzer Division east of Tula and the required link with XLIII Corps}}

The supporting march also slipped behind the attack. Guderian records the 296th Infantry Division reaching the Yasnaya Polyana area on 4 December without being able to attack that day. Its movement was supposed to help close Tula from the south and west; its absence from the immediate assault left the advanced troops waiting for a link that had not been made. This was a supply and movement problem expressed through the whereabouts of a named formation. Infantry that appeared in the plan as available support was still struggling toward the place where the plan required it to fight.{{p5-guderian-flanks::4 December: difficult march of the 296th Infantry Division to Yasnaya Polyana and no attack that day}}

Cold worsened these weaknesses, but the sequence must remain clear. The final offensive had lost momentum through fighting, attrition, and supply strain before the most severe early-December conditions described in German reports. Frozen ground had earlier helped vehicles move after the mud. Later cold and snow imposed different difficulties, including starting engines, handling weapons, sheltering troops, and evacuating wounded men. Weather did not act as a single event that suddenly converted a victorious army into a defeated one. Its effects accumulated within an already deteriorating military situation.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 51–53 and 63–65: changing weather and operational effects}}

The Soviet soldier did not escape these physical conditions. Moving on foot, waiting for supplies, and attacking exposed positions remained demanding even with more suitable clothing or equipment. The Soviet advantage lay partly in where and how additional forces could be committed, and in the Germans' growing inability to meet all the resulting threats. Treating one army as immune to winter would remove the human effort involved in the Soviet response. The relevant comparison concerns readiness and capacity under shared conditions, rather than one side possessing endurance by nationality.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–60 and 65–67: Soviet movement, readiness and uneven early counterattacks}}

<h2 id="reserves-that-had-to-arrive">Reserves that had to arrive</h2>

The Tenth Army provides a particularly clear example of the difference between formation and availability. Under Filipp Golikov, it had been assembling around Syzran on the Volga. The U.S. Army history describes seven reserve rifle divisions and approximately one hundred thousand troops, while emphasizing the shortage of motor vehicles. After receiving marching orders on 24 November, the army had to cover more than three hundred miles by rail and on foot. Those figures describe an army in movement, not one hundred thousand fully equipped men already standing in a line east of Tula.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 60 and note 41: Tenth Army, Syzran and movement orders}}

Golikov's destination placed Tenth Army against the eastern side of Guderian's position, beyond the area in which German patrols had tried to cut the Ryazan–Kolomna railway. That failure now mattered. A German intention to interrupt reinforcement had not become a securely severed route, and Soviet formations continued approaching the threatened sector. Tenth Army's journey was still incomplete, with troops moving by rail and on foot rather than arriving as a fully motorized concentration. The two armies' difficulties therefore intersected: Guderian had failed to shut the eastern approaches, while Golikov had to bring enough of his slowly arriving force into action to exploit that opening.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 60: Tenth Army deployment and limited motor transport}}{{p5-guderian-flanks::27 November: failure of Tenth Motorized Division’s detachments to reach the Ryazan–Kolomna railway}}

Stalin retained control over the commitment of additional reserve armies east of Moscow. Some were held for defence if the situation worsened, or for developing a counteroffensive if that became possible. This was a significant restraint compared with earlier moments when new forces had been committed rapidly into unsuccessful attacks. It did not mean the Soviet leadership possessed certainty about the outcome. The reserves were insurance against a continuing danger as well as a means of changing the battle. Their allocation reflected the need to preserve both possibilities.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 60–62: reserve armies and Stavka control over their use}}

The Soviet plans also reveal disagreement in emphasis. Zhukov's later account presents the German forces as badly depleted and the counteroffensive as a developing opportunity. Vasilevsky's account emphasizes urgency: active operations were necessary to halt the danger before it was too late. The U.S. Army history juxtaposes these interpretations rather than resolving them into a single confident decision. Both can contain part of the situation. Soviet commanders saw German weakness while still confronting attacks capable of causing serious local damage.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 61–62: Zhukov, Vasilevsky and differing descriptions of the counteroffensive decision}}

The transfer of authority on 29 November was a concrete step in turning reserves into a coordinated response. After Zhukov assured Stalin that major fresh German forces were unlikely to enter the battle immediately, Stalin placed the First Shock, Twentieth, and Tenth armies under Zhukov’s control for a counterattack. This did not complete their assembly or determine the outcome of every engagement. It gave the front commander responsibility for combining their actions with those of the armies already fighting around Moscow. The decision also rested on an assessment of German capacity: Soviet leaders were judging how much risk they could accept while moving from immediate containment toward a broader effort to recover the initiative.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 60 and note 42: Stalin’s transfer of three armies to Zhukov on 29 November}}

<h2 id="the-capital-behind-the-positions">The capital behind the positions</h2>

Moscow's defence had mobilized civilians as well as armies. During the October crisis, civilians, many of them women, had worked on defensive lines, while militia formations were organized for the city's protection. Those preparations remained part of the landscape the Germans were now approaching. Fieldworks did not defend themselves, but they could give troops cover, obstruct vehicles, and make an attacker deploy under fire. Civilian labour had therefore contributed to the time and effort required of the German formations in late November and early December.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 37–39: civilian construction and militia organization}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 53: defended fieldworks on the northern approaches}}

The continuing existence of a functioning capital also mattered institutionally. Government offices had been partly evacuated, industry was disrupted, and families faced separation, yet command and transport systems continued to support the defence. It would be misleading to make that continuity appear effortless. The autumn evacuation and economic losses had imposed severe strain. The more significant point is that disruption did not become the complete administrative collapse on which repeated German expectations had depended. Soviet institutions could still organize movement, allocate reserves, and sustain resistance under conditions of extraordinary loss.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 37–40: evacuation and industrial disruption}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–62: continued reserve organization}}

<aside class="history-aside"><p class="eyebrow">The misleading view from a map</p><p>Stahel draws attention to the impression created by headquarters maps at a scale of one to a million: German panzer groups appeared poised close to Moscow. At that scale, the distance remaining could look modest while the weakness of the formations was invisible. A position symbol did not show how many men were fit, how many vehicles would start, or how much ammunition could be delivered. The geographic proximity was real; the implication of an imminent decisive blow was an interpretation that required evidence the map could not supply.{{p5-stahel-gates::Public opening extract: headquarters maps and the deceptive impression of proximity}}</p></aside>

Geographical proximity alone could give the offensive a deceptive appearance of completion. The formations near Krasnaya Polyana and the canal approaches were close to Moscow, but they still faced organized Soviet troops and depended on long, threatened connections to the rear. Reports of an isolated patrol or a distant sighting cannot establish that a force capable of occupying the capital had reached the same point. The assessment here rests on the documented positions and actions of the main formations. They show an army that could still cause local crises, while repeatedly failing to supply the next movement on which its larger objective depended.{{p5-stahel-gates::Public opening extract: critique of proximity as evidence of operational capability}}{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 62–64: documented formation-level operations}}

Economic historian Mark Harrison emphasizes that evacuation was an improvised process with competing costs. Moving a factory too early interrupted production; waiting too long risked losing it. Railways carried military supplies toward a changing front and industrial equipment in the opposite direction, often toward destinations unprepared to receive it. These were constraints on the Soviet response as real as the German transport crisis. Soviet survival depended on managing them sufficiently well to keep producing and fighting, not on possessing a frictionless centrally planned machine.{{p4-harrison-industry::Industry and economy, manuscript pp. 9–10: timing, rail transport and unprepared evacuation destinations}}

The outcome was uneven readiness on both sides. German formations that had advanced across the western Soviet Union now lacked the men or fuel to exploit their remaining opportunities. Soviet headquarters were receiving new formations whose equipment and transport often lagged behind their manpower. This was not a contest between one perfectly functioning army and another that had simply stopped. The advantage was shifting because the Soviet command could introduce additional organized forces at threatened points while Bock had fewer means of responding. The incompleteness of those reinforcements helps explain the risks of the coming counterattack; their existence helps explain why it could be attempted.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 59–62: reserve readiness and commitment}}

<h2 id="choosing-how-to-stop">Choosing how to stop</h2>

By 3 December, Bock was explaining his persistence partly through fear of the alternative. Remaining on the offensive seemed preferable to adopting a defensive posture with weakened troops in exposed positions. That argument captures a trap created by the advance itself. The army had moved into ground it could not comfortably hold, yet withdrawing or preparing a coherent defence required decisions that would acknowledge the failure of its immediate aims. Continuing to attack postponed that acknowledgement while consuming the resources needed for the transition.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 63: Bock's explanation to Jodl}}

The Soviet command sought to strike during precisely that transition, before the Germans could regroup and establish a more secure winter line. Preparation for the broader counteroffensive intensified over the following days. Its opening would be staggered, with attacks on 5 December and wider operations on 6 December. Those developments belong to the [concluding Phase V study](/articles/barbarossa-exhausted-1941-12-05). On 2 December, the immediate picture remained one of overlapping attempts: German local attacks seeking to revive a failing offensive, and Soviet actions intended to remove the remaining threat and seize the initiative.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 63–67: Soviet timing, objectives and successive opening dates}}

The limits of the advance were therefore already visible before every German attack had ended. The pincer movements had failed to produce the intended convergence; local breakthroughs could not be sustained; reserves and transport had been depleted; and the Soviet state continued putting organized forces into the battle. The Germans remained dangerous, as the 258th Division's penetration showed. They no longer possessed a convincing means of turning that danger into the strategic result for which Barbarossa had been launched. The campaign's exhaustion was a relationship between objectives, available power, and an opponent still able to act.{{p5-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 61–67: remaining German attacks and Soviet capacity}}
