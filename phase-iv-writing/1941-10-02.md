<h2 id="a-plan-becomes-a-battle">A plan becomes a battle</h2>

Two days after the southern wing of Operation Typhoon began moving, the main force of Army Group Centre attacked on 2 October 1941. The distinction established in *The Offensive Begins* now becomes the organizing fact of this chapter. The German effort was no longer a threat developing on one flank. Mobile groups and infantry armies struck across the central approaches to Moscow, attempting to break through the Soviet Western and Reserve Fronts and then turn toward one another around Vyazma. Guderian's formation continued its separate sweep against the Bryansk Front to the south. The plan was a set of related operations, not one armoured column following the Moscow road.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, the 2 October opening and three panzer-group roles}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–80, German preparations and the Roslavl, Velizh and southern axes}}

The German command wanted a decisive destruction of the Soviet field armies before the capital. A city can be approached along a road, but an enemy army must be fixed, bypassed and prevented from withdrawing. That difference explains the shape of Typhoon. The northern pincer was to move from the Velizh sector, the southern part of the Vyazma pincer from the Roslavl region, and the wider right wing toward Orel. Infantry armies were essential to hold the shoulders of the breakthroughs and deal with forces left behind. German leaders expected success in this first stage to create a new strategic situation; the destruction of Soviet armies was not an incidental task on the way to the city.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–79, purpose of the encirclements and planned pursuit after their completion}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 1–2, purpose and scale of Typhoon}}

The result was still unknown when the attack opened. In later histories, arrows from both sides of Vyazma meet so readily that the danger to the Soviet fronts can seem preordained. On 2 October the German groups had to breach defended positions, maintain speed, find roads and crossings, keep their flanks from being cut, and meet at a place their commanders had marked as an objective. The Soviet fronts had to identify the decisive penetrations quickly enough to move reserves and withdraw or defend in useful directions. The story of the day is the beginning of that contest. The subsequent encirclements belong to the next chapter, where their closure dates and continuing resistance can be assessed rather than imported into this opening.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, first-day disposition and later development of the pockets}}

<h2 id="the-assembled-german-force">The assembled German force</h2>

Field Marshal Fedor von Bock commanded a concentration unprecedented for a single German army-group headquarters in this war. David Stahel's study places about 1.9 million men under Army Group Centre and identifies seventy-five divisions, including fourteen panzer divisions, in the enlarged force assembled for Typhoon. Its public introduction describes more than 1,500 tanks and roughly 1,000 aircraft assigned to the offensive. Such figures establish the scale of the operation, not the number of serviceable tanks that crossed each start line at dawn. They include formations with different tasks and strengths. The force was powerful precisely because it combined armour, motorized troops, infantry, artillery and air support across a very wide front.{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 1–2, Army Group Centre's formations and approximate equipment totals}}

The northern arm drew on Third Panzer Group under Hermann Hoth and Ninth Army. The central and southern arm against the Vyazma position combined Fourth Panzer Group under Erich Hoepner with Fourth Army. Farther south, Guderian's Second Panzer Group and Second Army were assigned the Bryansk–Orel problem. These formations did not carry identical loads or attack identical terrain. The armoured groups aimed for operational depth; the armies of marching infantry had to maintain the battle's breadth and deal with surviving Soviet positions. A successful panzer thrust therefore created more work for the troops following it. The German design required these parts to move in relation to one another even as terrain and resistance made their rates of advance unequal.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, the attacking panzer groups and infantry-army relationship}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–79, German assignment of the three mobile thrusts}}

The preparation itself exposed a weakness. Fourth Panzer Group's projected force around Roslavl included divisions sent from reserve and other army groups; Third Panzer Group near Velizh also received units transferred from the north. The German planning study lists the 10th Panzer Division already near Roslavl and identifies the 2nd, 5th, 11th, 19th and 20th as part of that southern Vyazma concentration. It places the 7th, 1st and 6th in the projected northern force. These lists describe planned assignments, not identical fighting strength or proof that every division moved on 2 October. Some units had travelled hundreds of miles across the front simply to be ready. Their movement consumed fuel and vehicle life before the offensive began.{{blau-planning::Printed p. 76, projected divisional assignments and difficulties of lateral movement}}

<h2 id="the-soviet-line-is-not-one-line">The Soviet line is not one line</h2>

Opposite Bock stood a Soviet system of three fronts rather than an undifferentiated mass. Ivan Konev commanded the Western Front; Semyon Budenny held the Reserve Front; Andrei Eremenko commanded the Bryansk Front in the south. The U.S. Army history gives their combined strength at no less than 1.25 million. That number shows a substantial rebuilding effort after the summer's losses. It should not be read as 1.25 million soldiers equally available to meet one German breach. Fronts covered different sectors, and their armies were separated by distances, transport requirements and command boundaries. The German plan deliberately sought to exploit those divisions in the Soviet position.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, defending fronts, commanders and approximate aggregate strength}}

The geography west of Moscow encouraged a dangerous simplification. Place-names such as Vyazma and Bryansk can make it sound as though the Soviet armies were posted in two compact circles waiting to be surrounded. In fact, commanders were defending routes, sectors and depth positions across a broad region. The Vyazma plan concerned forces west of the city and connections among Western and Reserve Front armies; the southern movement sought to disrupt Bryansk Front and the ways it could retreat or be reinforced. The German objective was to turn a dispersed defensive arrangement into isolated concentrations. On the first day, that transformation remained a purpose, not a fact.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, position of Western and Bryansk Fronts before the pockets formed}}

The Soviet command was neither ignorant of danger nor able to see the future. It knew that a major German effort might follow the fighting in Ukraine and had held and formed fronts to meet threats west and southwest of the capital. Yet the precise combination of German penetrations had to be established from reports arriving under attack. A headquarters might learn of contact along a road before it knew whether the blow was a local breakthrough, a feint or part of a larger envelopment. The task was especially difficult because the south had already been under attack for two days. Here, too, the historian must distinguish a documented disposition from an inferred thought in a commander's mind.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 33–36, Soviet preparation and the staggered German opening}}

The defenders were also a mixture of formations with different histories. Some had fought through summer battles; others had been raised or rebuilt during the pause in the centre. Experience and numbers did not always point in the same direction. A veteran formation might have learned how to survive German fire yet lack men and weapons. A newly assembled unit might be numerically large but short of training or transport. The source material available here does not justify a single quality score for all three fronts. It does justify the conclusion that the German plan was aimed at a living military system whose capacity to react varied by sector and day. The chapters that follow will test those reactions against specific movements, rather than declaring the Soviet army destroyed because a first-day breakthrough succeeded.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, Soviet rebuilding and German attack against the three fronts}}

Command labels can obscure the problem as much as aggregate totals. A “front” was a Soviet command responsible for several armies, not a single trench line; an “army” could contain divisions of unequal strength. The map's neat bands are an aid to orientation, but they cannot tell the reader how quickly a command could pass an order, whether a road between two sectors remained usable or what a subordinate had actually learned. Later Soviet changes of command should not be backdated to make the defense seem more unified on 2 October. In particular, Zhukov's later appointment to command the combined Western and Reserve Fronts belongs to the crisis that followed the opening attack, not to its first morning.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, Soviet command changes after the initial encirclement}}

<h2 id="the-attack-from-two-directions">The attack from two directions</h2>

The northern and central attacks on 2 October unfolded on approaches with different relationships to Vyazma. Hoth's Third Panzer Group moved from the Velizh region toward the northern side of the intended enclosure. Hoepner's Fourth Panzer Group struck from the Roslavl sector toward the southern side. Ninth and Fourth Armies supported the larger design with infantry and artillery. The advancing groups were not heading straight east in parallel; each had to turn into a position from which it could close behind Soviet armies. A report of forward progress from one wing therefore did not prove that the other had kept pace. The pincer metaphor is useful only while we remember that each arm consisted of men, vehicles and headquarters fighting on separate roads.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–79, assembly areas and the Vyazma envelopment plan}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, opening movements of Third and Fourth Panzer Groups}}

Guderian's right wing had a different geometry. His formation had begun on 30 September and now continued its north-eastern sweep toward Orel, while the Bryansk Front faced the prospect of being separated from the defenders closer to Moscow. The German planning study describes Second Panzer Group's intended route from Glukhov toward Orel and its possible continuation toward the capital if the first movement succeeded. That conditional clause matters. The ability to continue depended on defeating or bypassing Soviet forces, securing the roads and sustaining the force after its movement. A later map of German control cannot be laid over 2 October and called the opening situation.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 76–79, Guderian's Glukhov–Orel route and conditional further advance}}

The Luftwaffe was part of this combined operation. Stahel's estimate of roughly 1,000 aircraft committed to the offensive indicates the scale of the air effort, but an aircraft on an operational inventory was not continuously above each battlefield. Air support depended on weather, serviceability, airfield distance, communications with ground formations and decisions about priority. German aircraft could strike Soviet troops or transport and help mobile units break through; they could not replace infantry tasked with holding ground or vehicles carrying supplies. The same caution applies to Soviet air activity: the balance in the air was important, but it did not remove the Soviet fronts' ability to resist on the ground.{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed p. 2, approximate aircraft commitment to Typhoon}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, mobile and infantry composition of the offensive}}

Artillery and infantry preparation mattered even when armoured movement attracted the headlines. Tanks seeking an opening needed enemy strongpoints suppressed or bypassed, engineers to deal with obstacles and bridges, and roads kept sufficiently clear for the columns behind them. Infantry armies could pin defenders in place while mobile formations searched for depth; they could also be left with the harder work of fighting encircled troops after the vehicles had driven on. In that relationship lay both the power and the vulnerability of Typhoon. If the mobile units moved too slowly, defenders might escape the intended enclosure. If they moved too quickly without support, the route back to fuel and ammunition could remain insecure. A first-day report of miles gained could not settle which risk was emerging.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–80, relationship of mobile envelopments and central frontal attack}}

<h2 id="expectation-and-doubt">Expectation and doubt</h2>

German public messaging framed Typhoon as a decisive blow. Stahel's published chapter summary describes a proclamation issued by Hitler on the night of 1 October for reading to troops before the attack. It recounted enormous prisoner and equipment totals from the summer and announced another decisive battle. The figures were part of a political address intended to shape expectation; they should not be used here as verified measurements of Soviet losses. Its emphasis on what the army had already achieved also betrayed a problem: if so much had been destroyed, why was another campaign on the same scale necessary? Soviet resistance had outlived repeated German announcements of success.{{p4-stahel-opening::Cambridge public chapter 2 summary, Hitler's 1 October proclamation and its claims}}

Private reactions were less certain than the proclamation. Some soldiers questioned whether another victory could produce the political result promised to them. Such doubts coexisted with the army's capacity to carry out the attack: fatigue and scepticism did not prevent soldiers from fighting, just as a confident order did not make their vehicles reliable. The important contrast was between the scale of what they were being asked to do and the increasingly uncertain meaning of success. Since June, large Soviet losses had repeatedly been followed by new resistance. Typhoon asked the troops to believe that this time the relationship would change.{{p4-stahel-opening::Public chapter 2 summary, contrasting soldiers' reactions to the proclamation}}

The economic warning did not remove the immediate military opportunity. German commanders could reasonably see the concentration of Soviet armies west of Moscow as a target vulnerable to their mobile formations. The difficulty was in the next inference: that destroying those armies would remove the possibility of organized Soviet resistance. Thomas's assessment made that leap harder to justify. His conclusion was that German operations through the end of September had not decisively disabled the Soviet military economy. The field command was therefore opening a battle whose tactical design was more credible than the strategic timetable attached to it. Even a spectacular enclosure would leave Germany needing either further advances or a way to contain a state that still possessed productive territory.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 78–79, conclusion of Thomas's 2 October survey}}

Soviet leaders also had reason to distinguish hope from capacity. Their three fronts represented serious resistance, but the German command had assembled its greatest concentration at their expense. A front commander could not simply choose to defend every road equally. German breakthroughs demanded decisions about reserves and communications at a rate the Soviet command system might struggle to match. The evidence in the U.S. Army synthesis is strongest on the deployment and later result; it does not reproduce the moment-by-moment conversations in every Soviet headquarters on 2 October. We should therefore describe the institutional challenge without inventing a speech by Konev, Budenny or Eremenko at the first report of the assault.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, defending front commanders and the subsequent operational crisis}}

The difference was felt by soldiers as well as economists. Wolf Dose, serving in the German 58th Infantry Division, recorded disbelief that the Soviet Union would capitulate merely because Hitler had announced another decisive battle. His unit belonged to the northern theatre, so his diary is evidence of a wider German soldier's response rather than an eyewitness report from Bock's opening assault. Stahel uses it to illuminate the distance between official confidence and individual expectation. Dose had been given a promise of finality; he could not see the mechanism by which that promise would become true. His doubt did not make him representative of every soldier, but it makes the confident language of German headquarters less uniform than it appears in the proclamation.{{p4-stahel-opening::Public chapter 2 summary, Wolf Dose of 58th Infantry Division and his reaction to Hitler's proclamation}}

<h2 id="the-reach-of-supply">The reach of supply</h2>

The enlarged army group had to be supplied as an enlarged army group. Concentration put more tanks, guns and men into the central theatre, but also increased demand for fuel, ammunition, food, repairs and evacuation of casualties. The German planning account explicitly describes difficulties in moving mobile divisions laterally into position, in some cases across several hundred miles. The effort used up vehicles and rail capacity before those divisions had produced an operational gain. This is a more concrete explanation of pre-offensive strain than a retrospective claim that German forces simply lacked winter clothing. On 2 October the immediate question was whether a fast breakthrough could be fed and supported long enough to turn penetration into encirclement.{{blau-planning::Printed p. 76, long lateral transfers for the mobile groups}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 2–4, material erosion before and during Typhoon}}

On the very day the main offensive opened, General Georg Thomas submitted an economic assessment that challenged its promise of finality. Thomas headed the armed forces' economic and armaments organization. His survey considered successive hypothetical German advances, asking how much productive capacity the Soviet Union would retain after each. The analysis did not assume that taking Moscow would automatically end the war. Even extensive losses of industrial territory could leave the Soviet state able to make urgently needed equipment and continue resistance. This was an internal German calculation, not Soviet propaganda. It reveals that the leadership had access to arguments against equating another successful military operation with the destruction of the enemy's capacity to fight.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 78–79, General Thomas's military-economic survey of 2 October}}

Thomas's scenarios moved outward from a line embracing Moscow, Leningrad, Tula and Kharkov to additional losses in the Donets Basin and at the Maikop oil fields. A further case included Gorky, with its importance to vehicle and aircraft manufacture. The final scenario considered the loss of territory west of the Volga. These were analytical cases rather than positions held or an approved timetable of conquest. In the most extensive case, Thomas still did not regard economic collapse as certain without the loss of the Urals. The argument was consequential because Bock's offensive operated within a much narrower physical horizon. Winning west of Moscow might damage the Soviet economy severely without reaching the industrial resources that could sustain another round of armies.{{blau-planning::Printed p. 79, four hypothetical territorial-loss scenarios and the Urals qualification}}

The supply problem was reciprocal. Soviet commanders could use rail routes and roads to bring reserves west or pull threatened formations back, but they had to move under enemy air attack and with incomplete knowledge of which routes were still open. Destroying a railway or taking a junction had consequences for civilian evacuation as well as military reinforcement. Conversely, a damaged German advance route could slow a panzer group even while its nearest Soviet opponent was in retreat. These were not secondary details to be added after the “real” battle. The ability to connect force to place and time determined how much of the plan either side could execute.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–40, breakthrough, pursuit and later transport constraints}}

<h2 id="allied-promises-and-battlefield-time">Allied promises and battlefield time</h2>

The international dimension had advanced since the negotiations described on 30 September. Harriman reported from Moscow on 1 October that the British and American missions had reached an understanding with Stalin about supplies. His telegram said material within the agreed lists would be made available and that transportation had been discussed without a firm American commitment. He described an enthusiastic close to the conference. That was a diplomatic achievement at the moment Typhoon opened, but an understanding at a conference was not a shipment at the front. The armies west of Moscow still had to confront the immediate German concentration with the weapons they possessed or could move from Soviet stocks.{{p4-frus787::FRUS 1941, vol. I, document 787, Harriman telegram of 1 October on the supply understanding and transportation qualification}}

The distinction is especially important because German and Soviet leaders were judging time differently. Bock's operation sought a result before its own striking power and the season imposed further limits. Moscow's government needed to survive long enough for its mobilization and prospective external assistance to count. Neither side could know the actual delivery timetable or military effect of every promised item. The next day's fighting would test the German timetable first: could mobile formations pass through the front before Soviet armies altered their dispositions? The October protocol had political and logistical significance, but this chapter does not assign it tactical effects it had not yet produced.{{p4-frus787::FRUS 1941, vol. I, document 787, scope and limits of the October supply understanding}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 1–4, German need for rapid strategic decision}}

The British and American missions also reveal a limit to a purely national account of Moscow's defense. The Soviet Union was doing the fighting on this front, and the decisive immediate resources were Soviet soldiers, production and transport. At the same time, Soviet leaders were actively seeking material from powers whose factories and shipping routes lay far from the battlefield. Their partners could make commitments that mattered in a long war but could not abolish the distance between a London or Washington decision and a gun outside Vyazma. A detailed military narrative should keep both propositions in view. Ignoring the conference erases a contemporaneous Soviet priority; treating its outcome as an instant reinforcement erases the practical problem that diplomacy was trying to solve.{{p4-frus787::FRUS 1941, vol. I, document 787, commitments and unresolved transportation}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 38–39, lag between allied requests and battlefield delivery}}

<h2 id="the-first-day-and-its-unfinished-work">The first day and its unfinished work</h2>

By the end of 2 October, Army Group Centre had committed its main force. The attackers had the initiative, the advantage of a prepared concentration and a plan aimed at the links between Soviet fronts. The defenders retained substantial forces and the possibility of organized reaction, but the speed and direction of the attack threatened to prevent them from using those forces as a coherent whole. This is the moment at which numbers become less explanatory than movement. Two large armies could appear roughly comparable in aggregate while one was using mobile formations to cut behind the other's sector headquarters and the other was still trying to identify which reports described the decisive danger.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, comparative strengths and opening envelopment}}

No pocket was complete on this date. The German operational objective was to close around Soviet armies; it was not a fact to be drawn backward from the maps of later days. Nor was Moscow an undefended prize beyond the first line. Stahel's introduction reminds us that operational success and strategic victory were different achievements. The German army had already won enormous battles without compelling Soviet surrender, and any new encirclement would still leave questions of pursuit, supply, Soviet reserves and political will. The main assault made those questions urgent. It did not answer them.{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 1–4, distinction between tactical-operational victory and strategic outcome}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, later closure of the Vyazma and Bryansk pockets}}

The next dated study turns to 7 October, when the encirclement battles around Vyazma and Bryansk had become the central reality of the campaign. Between these two dates German units would advance, Soviet commands would attempt to hold or recover lines, and the geometry of escape would change rapidly. To understand the resulting disaster, we must follow both the speed of the German points and the slower process of closing, guarding and reducing the spaces behind them. The plan described here began with a violent surprise, but a plan could become a result only through sustained fighting. That is why the chapter ends at an opening rather than at a verdict.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, sequence from the 2 October attack to the encirclement battles}}
