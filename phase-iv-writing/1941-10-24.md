<h2 id="a-southern-war">A southern war with its own clock</h2>

On 24 October 1941, German Sixth Army took Kharkov, the industrial and railway city in eastern Ukraine. The event occurred while Moscow remained under an emergency regime and Army Group Centre struggled along its own approach roads. Neither development was a subordinate footnote to the other. Army Group South, commanded by Gerd von Rundstedt, pursued a cluster of objectives: Kharkov, the Donets industrial basin, the route toward Rostov and the entrance to the Crimea. Its armies did not move at the same speed or along a single axis. The capture of Kharkov was a clear change of control on this date, but it did not mean that the entire Soviet southern front collapsed or that Germany had gained the city's evacuated machinery.{{p4-judaica-kharkov::Encyclopaedia Judaica, Holocaust and Modern Periods, 24 October occupation date}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–35 and 39–40, Army Group South objectives and late-October advances}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, Sixth Army and separate southern axes}}

The preceding chapter examined the Soviet government's attempt to keep Moscow governable. Kharkov posed another kind of survival question: how to preserve military production when a manufacturing center lay on the route of an invading army. The German aim was not merely a place name on a map. Kharkov had factories, skilled workers and rail connections that could support Soviet armies; taking it might disrupt their production and communications. The Soviet response combined delaying combat with removal of industrial equipment. Those efforts could succeed in part even if the city was lost. Conversely, a train moving a factory east did not instantly create a replacement factory at the destination. A dated military narrative has to track both the urban battle and the longer movement of productive capacity.{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed pp. 1 and 10, wartime production network and uneven factory restoration}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, Kharkov as German objective}}

An atlas should show the southern operations in relation to Moscow without flattening them into one broad arrow across the Soviet Union. German Second Panzer Army at the Tula approaches belonged to Army Group Centre's campaign. German Sixth Army at Kharkov belonged to Army Group South. Both threatened Soviet rail and industrial systems, and the Soviet high command had to judge where scarce reserves were most needed. But their immediate commanders, lines of supply and defenders were different. Even the weather did not affect all roads simultaneously. By 24 October the war in the east was a series of connected resource decisions fought on distinct terrain.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–40, distinct army-group missions and simultaneous threats}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, separate central and southern movement in late October}}

<h2 id="from-kiev-to-kharkov">From Kiev to Kharkov</h2>

The great German victory around Kiev in September had damaged Soviet Southwest Front catastrophically. It also required time to process captives, reorganize German formations and push supply beyond the Dnepr. In the following offensive design, Hitler returned the main concentration of panzer strength to Army Group Centre for Typhoon. Army Group South still had to move east, with Sixth Army toward Kharkov, Seventeenth Army into the Donets region, First Panzer Army farther south toward Rostov, and Eleventh Army contesting the Crimea. The redistribution left the southern theater with a difficult set of tasks over a broad front rather than an unlimited mobile reserve.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 33–35, Directive 35's division of central and southern tasks}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Sixth and Seventeenth Armies and Panzer Advance to Rostov, delay after Kiev and distinct missions}}

Walther von Reichenau's Sixth Army advanced through the northern part of this theater. The Seventeenth Army, whose command passed to Hermann Hoth in October, moved farther south and east. Ewald von Kleist's First Panzer Army pursued a faster coastal and lower-Don direction. These names describe commands, not equal bundles of immediately usable strength. Infantry armies depended heavily on horses and wagons and could not simply keep pace with an armoured spearhead. A panzer formation could move rapidly, then wait for fuel or for troops to protect its exposed flank. A corps committed to one road could not at the same hour clear a second railway junction. The southern campaign's tempo must be read through those differences.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Sixth and Seventeenth Armies and Panzer Advance to Rostov, commanders and differing routes}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, southern army-group movements and attrition}}

The Soviet response was no longer the defense of Kiev's giant salient. Semyon Timoshenko had been given overall responsibility for the southwestern theater after the earlier disaster, and the fronts had to decide which cities could be defended, which forces should withdraw and what equipment could be sent east. The units near Kharkov faced the German Sixth Army while the larger Soviet command watched threats in the Donets Basin and Crimea. The possibility of another vast pocket made holding ground at any cost especially dangerous. Kirchubel describes Soviet withdrawal decisions designed to avoid a new encirclement at Kharkov. That does not make the defense token or the loss insignificant; it shows that preservation of forces and industrial equipment could be a different objective from permanent possession of the city.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–35, Timoshenko's southwestern command and German objectives}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, Soviet withdrawal to avoid another encirclement}}

The difference between a mobile and a sustained advance was visible behind the front. German troops crossing the Dnepr could not feed an army from the far bank without bridges, pontoon routes or converted rail lines. Kirchubel describes damaged bridges, ice threatening temporary crossings and railway-gauge work that did not yet put European-gauge service close enough to every forward unit. These constraints did not halt all movement on 24 October. They explain why a command could take a major city yet struggle to send adequate supply to the formations needed for the next objective. This southern logistics problem paralleled that outside Moscow but played out through the Dnepr crossings and Ukraine's long eastward transport corridors.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, Dnepr crossing and rail-gauge limitations}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, attrition and supply conditions in Army Group South}}

<h2 id="kharkov-on-the-twenty-fourth">Kharkov on the twenty-fourth</h2>

The German Sixth Army's seizure of Kharkov on 24 October is independently recorded by the U.S. Army's general campaign narrative as part of the army group's late-October advance and by the Encyclopaedia Judaica entry as a specific date. Kirchubel places the army under Reichenau and describes it approaching from the north while Seventeenth Army fought farther southeast. The agreement on the date is firm enough for a dated entry. The narrower details of exactly when each neighborhood fell or which small bridge changed hands require unit records that this study has not verified. We can describe the city's capture and the main approach, while avoiding a fictional street-by-street itinerary made from broad theater maps.{{p4-judaica-kharkov::Encyclopaedia Judaica, Holocaust and Modern Periods, German occupation on 24 October}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 40, Kharkov taken in the last week of October}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, Sixth Army's approach and capture}}

The urban fighting did not occur in an empty industrial landscape. Kharkov was a large city with workers, families, institutions and a substantial Jewish community. Some civilians had been evacuated; others remained because of work, care obligations, transport limitations or personal decisions. We do not have a credible source that assigns motives to all who stayed. The Encyclopaedia Judaica entry records that many of the city's Jews managed to leave before German occupation, a fact that testifies to both the perceived danger and the existence of escape routes for some. Those who remained faced German rule; their subsequent persecution belongs to the occupation chronology and must not be collapsed into a claim that every later killing occurred on 24 October.{{p4-judaica-kharkov::Encyclopaedia Judaica, Holocaust and Modern Periods, prewar Jewish community, evacuation and subsequent occupation}}

Control of a city also meant control of a rail junction. Railways out of Kharkov could potentially serve German operations toward the Donets region and Soviet withdrawal routes farther east. But a captured station was not automatically a working German supply hub. Tracks might be damaged, rolling stock removed and gauge conversion unfinished; fuel had to reach the station before trucks could distribute it to troops. The Soviet defenders and evacuation authorities tried to deny Germany the immediate use of productive machinery, while the Germans needed the roads and rail lines to support their continued advance. The difference between possessing infrastructure and operating it is central to understanding the day.{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed pp. 1 and 10, industrial evacuation and interdependent transport}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, late-October German rail and supply difficulties}}

For Soviet soldiers defending the approaches, a withdrawal could be both a defeat and a means of preserving combat power. After the September destruction near Kiev, the loss of a further army in another enclosure would be especially dangerous. A unit ordered to delay German entry needed to judge how long a position could be held before its retreat road closed. Such judgments varied by unit and are not reducible to one heroic or cowardly decision. The record supports an organized Soviet effort to remove equipment and to avoid a second vast trap. It does not support a precise claim that every soldier defending Kharkov escaped, nor that every industrial asset was saved. The city fell; the Soviet ability to continue fighting and producing depended on what had moved away before it did.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, Soviet withdrawal and capture}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed p. 10, varying results of factory evacuation}}

<h2 id="industry-as-a-moving-target">Industry as a moving target</h2>

The tractor factory offers a useful example of an industrial organization dispersed by evacuation. Research by the Kharkiv Historical Museum traces its equipment and personnel first through Stalingrad and then to Rubtsovsk, where they helped form the Altai tractor enterprise. This was a succession of moves and reorganizations rather than a working factory lifted intact to a new location. The museum dates the Altai plant's first tractor to August 1942, well beyond this chapter's horizon. That later result illustrates how misleading it would be to measure every evacuation by an exceptionally rapid resumption elsewhere. A trainload of machine tools could preserve future capacity while contributing nothing to immediate output. For the Soviet state, industrial survival and the maintenance of current production were urgent but different problems.{{p4-kharkiv-tractor::Tetyana Horlova, museum research paper, wartime evacuation through Stalingrad to Rubtsovsk and first Altai tractor on 24 August 1942}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Printed p. 10, receiving-region shortages and uneven restoration of production}}

An evacuated plant was more than a set of machine tools. Skilled workers knew tolerances, repair techniques and the informal solutions that let production continue when supplies were scarce. They needed housing and food at the destination. A Soviet army could have a nominal requirement for new tanks, but a dismantled and travelling factory could not meet it that week. Harrison's broader argument is that evacuation preserved the possibility of military survival while imposing major strains on transport, power and consumption in the interior. The RUDN Journal of Russian History's published abstract likewise emphasizes the difficulties and losses of armour-industry evacuation rather than treating removal as a frictionless achievement. Those later assessments explain the stakes of the Kharkov decision without erasing the disruption experienced by workers.{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed pp. 1 and 10, skilled labor, receiving-region shortages and uneven results}}{{p4-ermolov-industry::Accessible English abstract, difficulties and losses during armor-industry evacuation}}

This process complicates the phrase “Germany captured an industrial center.” The geographical fact is clear; the economic result depends on what remained, what could be repaired and whether workers stayed. Occupation could still give German forces housing, roads and a rail location while depriving the Soviet Union of a functioning city for some time. It could also expose the remaining population to coercion and requisition. Yet the German high command's hoped-for industrial gain and the Soviet state's actual production loss were not identical figures. A historian needs records of equipment removal and later operation to measure them. In their absence, it is better to describe the mechanisms and state what is securely documented.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, industrial and rail aims}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed p. 10, evacuation success and delayed restoration}}

For workers, the move was a disruption of an industrial community as well as of production. The Kharkiv museum's account describes the tractor factory's eastern transfer in stages, with equipment and personnel becoming part of a new enterprise far from the city. Such relocation demanded more than railway space: people needed somewhere to live, food, and the means to reassemble and operate the machinery. Those who left could remain connected to the national war effort while losing the familiar relationships of a neighbourhood and workplace. Those who stayed entered occupation with fewer productive resources around them. Saving industrial capacity therefore did not divide people neatly into those rescued and those abandoned; it redistributed both work and vulnerability across a much larger territory.{{p4-kharkiv-tractor::Museum research paper, wartime evacuation and formation of the Altai enterprise}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Printed p. 10, demands on labour, housing and local resources in receiving regions}}

<h2 id="donbas-rostov-and-crimea">Other southern directions</h2>

Kharkov was only one point in Army Group South's operations. Farther south, Seventeenth Army pressed through the Donets region against Soviet defenders whose retreat and local resistance made German progress uneven. First Panzer Army moved toward Taganrog and Rostov, with its mobile formations dependent on long supply corridors and flank protection. The U.S. Army's planning study places First Panzer Army in the Taganrog area by 20 October and describes stronger resistance and mud affecting Seventeenth and Sixth Armies. It also records attrition in Sixth Army. These broad placements support the picture of a wide offensive with different rates of advance; they do not identify every village or crossing held at noon on the twenty-fourth.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, First Panzer Army at Taganrog by 20 October and slower Sixth and Seventeenth Armies}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, separate Donbas and Rostov movements}}

The coal and industrial districts of the Donets Basin had strategic significance because the Soviet war effort needed fuel, metals, machinery and rail links. German command could threaten those resources by occupying ground, but the same questions of evacuation and operating capacity applied. Soviet troops and local workers had to decide what could be removed, defended or denied. Kirchubel describes worker militia joining regular Soviet forces in the region and German infantry employing local wagons when its own mobility lagged. Those details matter because they reveal a campaign that cannot be explained by tank arrows alone. Soldiers travelled and fought in a landscape worked by civilians whose mines, railways and factories were objectives of the war.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, worker militia and infantry transport in the Donbas}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed pp. 1 and 10, industry and transport in Soviet war production}}

In Crimea, Eleventh Army under Erich von Manstein faced a succession of narrow defensive positions. The October battle was for the Ishun positions south of Perekop, rather than the first entry into the Perekop defenses. The offensive there began on 18 October. Lakes and constricted passages reduced the space available for manoeuvre, while Soviet troops evacuated from Odessa helped reinforce the defenders. On 24 October the decisive penetration into the peninsula had not yet been completed. Sevastopol remained Soviet-held, and the later division of Soviet forces between the western fortress and the Kerch direction was still ahead. Treating all of Crimea as German territory would erase an active battle whose outcome mattered to both the Black Sea Fleet and the civilian population.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, The Crimea, Ishun offensive beginning 18 October and later breakthrough}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, continued Crimean resistance and later split toward Sevastopol and Kerch}}

The wide southern map also includes Odessa, occupied by Romanian forces on 16 October. By the twenty-fourth, Romanian authorities were perpetrating mass murder after the 22 October explosion at their headquarters. The Holocaust Museum separates the Romanian occupation, the explosion and the killings of 22–26 October, while noting a limited German security-unit role. These crimes were committed by identifiable authorities against civilians; they cannot be explained as a side effect of taking Kharkov, nor should they be omitted from a dated survey of the southern front. Their presence makes clear that Axis control had consequences far beyond moving a frontline symbol eastward. The main narrative here remains Kharkov, but the occupation of the Black Sea city was another part of the same week.{{p4-ushmm-odessa::USHMM Odessa, 16 October Romanian occupation and 22–26 October massacres}}

<h2 id="what-a-southern-advance-required">What a southern advance required</h2>

Taking a city did not end the work of an army. Sixth Army needed to place a garrison in Kharkov, protect the rail and road approaches, identify usable stores and repair damage before the position could support its next movement. Patrols and security units might secure a station while field formations faced Soviet troops beyond the eastern edge. The label “German-held” is appropriate for the city after its capture; it should not be read as meaning that every route radiating from it was safe or that Soviet forces had ceased to operate nearby. A commander who detached troops to hold a rail junction reduced the force immediately available for pursuit. A junction's military value grew only when it could carry the traffic for which it had been taken.{{p4-judaica-kharkov::Encyclopaedia Judaica, city captured 24 October}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, continuing Soviet withdrawal and German supply limits}}

Seventeenth Army's adjacent advance exposed the same arithmetic at a larger scale. Its infantry could maintain contact with Sixth Army or press more rapidly into the Donets region, but limited mobility and supply made those tasks compete. A panzer army farther south could run ahead toward Rostov, yet each mile increased the length of its northern flank until infantry could occupy the intervening ground. Kirchubel's account of Army Group South emphasizes precisely this mismatch between ambitious directions and the number of formations able to cover them. The operational map therefore needs space between army arrows: the interval was not empty in the sense that nobody lived there, but it was not a seamless German line either. Soviet troops might withdraw through it, attack a road or establish a new position.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov and Panzer Advance to Rostov, inter-army contact and flank exposure}}

The route east also depended on river crossings that remained vulnerable after the spearheads had passed. Traffic over a pontoon bridge could be delayed by ice or high water, but it could also be delayed by a queue of ammunition carts, wounded transports and fuel trucks moving in opposite directions. Engineers could restore capacity, yet their work took time and materials. The German planning history's general account of October emphasizes slowing movement; Kirchubel adds the Dnepr bridge problem in the south. Taken together, they suggest why the forward unit's pace was a poor measure of the whole army group's power. Sustained advance required the passage of mass, not only a reconnaissance car reaching the next town.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, widening gap between pursuit and supply}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, destroyed Dnepr bridges and pontoon constraints}}

The Soviet side made a converse calculation. A withdrawal from one threatened city could shorten the front and spare formations for another defense, but it meant abandoning residents and land to the occupier. Moving factory equipment could preserve long-term production while consuming trains needed for soldiers and food. Holding Perekop and Sevastopol could keep a Black Sea base and deny Germany a peninsula even as other ground in Ukraine was lost. None of these choices was costless, and there was no single headquarters report that could make them all work. The war in eastern Ukraine was consequently neither pure retreat nor pure German breakthrough. It was a contest over how much combat power and industrial capacity each state could carry into the next month.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–40, simultaneous Soviet southern tasks}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed p. 10, evacuation's uneven results}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, Crimea defense and southern attrition}}

There was also a less familiar item on German staff desks on this exact date. The postwar U.S. Army planning study records an exploratory 24 October discussion of a future Caucasus offensive, including oil fields and routes toward Iran. This was planning for a later campaign, not evidence that German troops were already advancing into the Caucasus or that Hitler had approved every step of the staff concept. Its importance to this chapter is limited but real: even as Kharkov fell and Army Group South struggled to sustain its present movement, planners looked beyond Rostov to objectives whose distance and logistical demands would be greater. The gap between staff possibility and operational means was growing. Readers should not mistake a meeting about what might be attempted for a field order executed on 24 October.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 111–113, 24 October exploratory Caucasus planning conference and stated prerequisites}}

The oil discussion also illustrates the difference between an economic objective and a usable resource. Seizing an oil field would not instantly fuel panzer units on the Dnepr or at Kharkov. Wells, refineries, pipelines, rail tank cars and security along the route would all matter. Nor would a Soviet commander be compelled to leave the equipment intact. In October, the immediate German fuel problem was more prosaic: move available stocks from supply heads across damaged transport corridors to formations already fighting. A distant strategic aspiration could not substitute for that flow. The staff's interest in Caucasus oil therefore helps explain the stakes of the southern drive while underlining how far the army remained from the means to realize it.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 111–113, Caucasus-oil concept and prerequisites}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, current fuel and transport constraints}}

<h2 id="the-strategic-balance">Why the advances did not settle the war</h2>

The German capture of Kharkov was an important success. It took a major Soviet city, widened German control in eastern Ukraine and threatened further movement along transport lines. Yet success at one node did not supply every army group or remove Soviet resistance elsewhere. Army Group Centre's attack toward Moscow still depended on roads and formations in its own theater. Army Group South's leaders still had to decide whether their remaining mobility should be spent on the Donets, the lower Don or support of infantry at Kharkov. Those choices had consequences for flank protection and supply. Kirchubel records disagreements over whether Seventeenth Army should help Sixth Army take Kharkov or continue its own advance. The eventual city capture did not erase the opportunity cost of those debates.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Kharkov to Rostov, debate over Sixth and Seventeenth Army cooperation}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–40, separate strategic objectives}}

The Soviet state was also fighting through choices rather than simply absorbing blows. Its troops withdrew to avoid a new enclosure and continued to defend on other southern axes; its industrial authorities sent equipment and workers east. These actions had different time horizons. A delaying battalion might preserve a rail departure that evening. An evacuated factory might produce nothing for weeks before becoming useful again. A defensive position in the Crimea could protect a fleet base long after Kharkov had fallen. The historian's duty is to connect the horizons without turning the eventual Soviet survival into an inevitability apparent to people on 24 October. They were making decisions amid losses, imperfect information and urgent material scarcity.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, southern Soviet withdrawal and separate axes}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed p. 10, interruption and uneven restoration after evacuation}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, Crimea fighting continuing into November}}

German success brought occupation. In Kharkov, residents faced the rule of an army whose war in the east was tied to racial persecution and economic extraction. The city entry in the Encyclopaedia Judaica records subsequent hostage taking and murder of Jews, followed by later ghettoization and mass killing. These later events should not be narrated as though all had happened on the capture day, but their prospect prevents a bloodless closing line about a rail junction acquired. Control of a city meant control over its population, homes, food and workplaces. The soldiers entering Kharkov did not merely pass a point on the road to Rostov. They began an occupation under Nazi Germany.{{p4-judaica-kharkov::Encyclopaedia Judaica, Holocaust and Modern Periods, later hostage taking and mass murder}}

On 24 October, then, the military map should mark Kharkov's fall, show the separate German directions across the Donbas and toward Rostov, and leave Sevastopol under Soviet control. It should also resist drawing a settled line around every spearhead. German movement, Soviet withdrawal and local resistance were still changing the southern front. The next study returns to the centre in early November, where the issue is whether the attacking armies could resume a coordinated drive after weeks of road, supply and manpower strain. Kharkov's fate is part of that larger resource contest: a city was lost, machinery moved, and the opposing armies still had a war to sustain.{{p4-judaica-kharkov::Encyclopaedia Judaica, 24 October capture}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, late-October southern movement and continuing Crimea battle}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed p. 10, evacuation and restoration context}}
