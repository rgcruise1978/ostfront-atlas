<h2 id="after-the-rings">After the rings closed</h2>

On 13 October 1941, the road to Moscow was not a single road and the Soviet defense was not a single line. Six days after German mobile forces closed the great enclosure at Vyazma, Soviet formations still fought behind the advancing spearheads. Farther south, the battle around Bryansk continued in broken pockets and forests. German commanders wanted to use their victory before a new defense could form. Soviet commanders, newly reorganized under Georgy Zhukov, tried to turn fragments of armies, arriving reserves, local garrisons and unfinished fieldworks into positions capable of delaying that exploitation. The capital was the strategic point of reference, but the fighting lay across a broad fan of northern, western and southwestern approaches.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, pocket fighting and Zhukov's Mozhaisk assignment}}

This chapter asks what it meant to build a front after the armies that had manned the previous one were encircled. The answer lies less in an abstract distance from Moscow than in the junctions through which people and supplies had to pass. Kalinin was a northern approach; Volokolamsk and Mozhaisk lay on routes from the west; Kaluga and the Orel–Mtsensk axis opened the southwest. German forces moved toward these places with different speeds and different tasks. Some remained tied to the prisoners and Soviet combatants behind them. A Soviet detachment could therefore lose a field position and still alter the timing of the campaign if it held a road junction long enough for another force to take position nearer the city.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–40, separate northern and southwestern German directions and Soviet delaying defense}}{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, the continued commitment of German units to the Vyazma perimeter}}

The scale of the preceding defeat must not be disguised. The German army had isolated major forces from the Soviet Western and Reserve Fronts and damaged Eremenko's Bryansk Front. Yet the phrase “the road stood open” compresses two different things: a breach in a field army and the ability of an attacking army to move through that breach, keep itself supplied and defeat the next troops it met. These were not identical. The German mobile formations had won striking operational successes; they also required infantry to hold perimeters, engineers to restore routes and transport columns to bring forward fuel and ammunition. The surviving Soviet command needed every hour of that process.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–40, encirclement results and the later slowing of pursuit}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 79–81, exploitation problems after the pocket battles}}

<h2 id="a-command-remade">A command remade in crisis</h2>

Zhukov assumed control of the combined Western and Reserve Fronts on 10 October. His appointment did not deliver a fresh army by itself. He inherited an enormous geographical responsibility at the moment when the fate of many divisions that had belonged to those fronts remained unclear. The U.S. Army's campaign history describes his assignment as manning the Mozhaisk defensive system with survivors from Vyazma, recent conscripts and seasoned troops transferred from other sectors. This was an improvised force assembled under fire. An order assigning a unit to a sector could say where it ought to stand; it did not prove that the unit had arrived with its guns, transport, signals equipment and a dependable ammunition supply.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, Zhukov's 10 October assumption of combined fronts and composition of the new defense}}

The Soviet high command faced a particularly hard problem in judging what remained of the encircled forces. A staff might list an army as lost, even while detached elements still fought or escaped. A commander appearing with a few hundred men could be useful, but he could not be counted as the whole formation that had existed before 2 October. Headquarters therefore had to distinguish paper strength from actual bodies able to hold a particular crossing or trench. It also had to choose which roads to defend first. The basic answer was to concentrate on the western approaches, especially those through Mozhaisk, while covering the flanks from Volokolamsk in the north to Kaluga and the approaches beyond in the south. This was a set of sectors and emergency priorities, not a continuous wall surrounding a secure interior.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, survivors and piecemeal manning of the Mozhaisk line}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 79–81, Soviet reorganization and German approach directions}}

The approach sectors gave the new defense a recognizable organization. Sixteenth Army was associated with Volokolamsk, Fifth with Mozhaisk, Forty-third with Maloyaroslavets and Forty-ninth with the Kaluga direction. Their assignments placed armies across the principal routes by which German forces might approach the capital. They did not create an equally strong barrier between every pair of towns. A position on the highway could be vital precisely because the neighbouring country was poorly suited to moving heavy weapons and supplies. Soviet commanders needed enough strength at these junctions to force German units to deploy for battle, while retaining the ability to reinforce another road if the enemy shifted its effort.{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, Moscow approaches, army assignments at Volokolamsk, Mozhaisk, Maloyaroslavets and Kaluga}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, improvised manning of the Mozhaisk defense}}

This was command under imperfect information, not simply a contest of will. Telephone and telegraph links had been broken by the advances and by the movement of headquarters. Reports about German tanks might reach Moscow after the tanks had turned onto another road. Orders to an army could be overtaken by its retreat or by a local counterattack. Zhukov's task was to make these uncertain reports actionable: place a reserve on an approach, establish a headquarters that could still communicate, and decide which sector could accept a withdrawal without exposing another. His appointment is often rendered as a decisive personal intervention. The more useful observation is institutional: a commander acquired authority over remnants that first had to become a coordinated defense.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, reconstitution of Western Front after Vyazma}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 3–5, Soviet resistance and German operational uncertainty}}

In mid-October it consisted of threatened corridors, incomplete works and units arriving at different times. <h2 id="north-and-west">The northern and western approaches</h2>

North of the direct Smolensk–Moscow highway, Third Panzer Group pressed toward the Kalinin region, seeking routes that could carry it beyond the Soviet western defense and threaten the capital from above. Kalinin was not yet a German conquest on 13 October: the U.S. Army history dates its fall to the following day. That one-day distinction is consequential. A dated map that shades the city German on the thirteenth would transfer a later achievement into the moment when Soviet commanders still had to respond to the threat. German movement toward Kalinin also stretched the operational geometry. A column advancing north could outflank a western position, but it moved farther from the most direct supply routes and had to keep roads open behind it.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, Third Panzer Group and Kalinin's capture on 14 October}}

Volokolamsk, on the northwestern approach, was one of the places in which a defense might anchor itself even if country between strongpoints remained thinly held. Farther south, Mozhaisk commanded the direct highway and railway corridor from Smolensk. These routes mattered because an army marching toward Moscow could not advance as a uniform wave through every forest and field. Tanks, heavy guns and supply trucks depended on passable roads and bridges. A railway offered large carrying capacity only after the tracks, gauge and forward unloading facilities could actually serve the force at the front. Soviet defense at a junction could therefore impose costs disproportionate to the length of trench held there. German units might go around it, but doing so demanded fuel, reconnaissance and a secure alternative route.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–40, approaches to Moscow and deterioration of roads}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, dependence of the October advance on highways and supply}}

The western front had another layer of danger. Men who had escaped Vyazma were arriving from the west at the same time that German infantry and armour drove east. A survivor was a trained defender, but he might lack his unit, heavy weapon or immediate commander. His knowledge of the enemy's route could be valuable and already out of date. When histories write that “survivors manned the line,” they describe a transition that required regrouping, identification and supply. Some troops would be fit for a position immediately. Others needed rest or re-equipment, and many never reached it. The fact of their continued resistance in the pocket had helped buy time; their subsequent usefulness could not be assumed from their mere appearance beyond the ring.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, survivors of the Vyazma pocket used in Mozhaisk defense}}{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, fighting around the eastern Vyazma perimeter}}

Hoepner's pursuit was already showing its material limits. The force sent toward Kaluga included the 20th Panzer Division, which reported only thirty-nine serviceable tanks on 11 October. On the Gzhatsk direction, *Das Reich* had suffered heavily in a Soviet tank attack the previous day. These were formations released from the Vyazma containment battle to exploit its success, yet neither could advance with undiminished strength. Their condition helps explain why the defeat of the Soviet armies west of Vyazma did not leave a fresh German mobile army beyond them. The forces needed for the next operation were the same forces already being consumed by combat and movement.{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, 20th Panzer Division return of 11 October and Soviet attack on Das Reich on 10 October}}

<h2 id="southwest-corridor">The long southwestern corridor</h2>

The southern arm of Typhoon should not be folded into the Mozhaisk story. Guderian's Second Panzer Group, redesignated Second Panzer Army early in October, had begun its offensive two days before the main Army Group Centre attack. Its advance through Orel and toward Mtsensk threatened the approaches to Tula and, beyond that, the possibility of turning around Moscow from the south. By the thirteenth, the armoured spearhead had travelled far beyond the line at which it opened the operation, but had not already captured Tula or completed the southern encirclement of the capital.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, Guderian's Orel–Orel–Mtsensk direction; no exact capture date asserted}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, Second Panzer Army designation and objectives toward Tula}}

The route crossed country in which rivers, woods and road surfaces made speed uneven. A retrospective German terrain study stressed the difficulty of moving through the Desna basin and reaching reliable roads before wet weather. Its observations illuminate transport geography but need careful use: the study was written from German officers' experience after the war, and its emphasis on terrain can obscure the Soviet units that fought on the approaches to Mtsensk. A road did not become unusable solely because it rained; fighting, demolition, traffic congestion and the need to bring forward artillery could all stop a column. An armored formation at its farthest point might be waiting for fuel and infantry while its leading scouts were still advancing.{{p4-cmh-terrain::Printed pp. 10–11, southern river and road geography and German transport concerns}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36 and 39–40, Soviet resistance and subsequent road conditions}}

The danger to Moscow from this direction was real because the city was also a rail and command hub for central Russia. German planners hoped to sever the routes by which Soviet armies could move reserves or supplies laterally. The existence of that objective did not mean it was already achieved. Tula remained a Soviet industrial and military center; its defense would become a separate test of whether Guderian's force could convert a rapid advance to sustained movement. Soviet troops on the Mtsensk axis could delay the German column even if they could not restore the original front. The time they gained mattered only when Soviet command used it to assemble men and material along the following corridors.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–40, Mtsensk approach, Tula direction and operational delay}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, German southern goals and emerging slowdown}}

Army Group Centre's southern advance also affected the map of the wider eastern front. A panzer army pushing toward Tula could not be treated as the same force attacking Kharkov, which lay in Army Group South's theater. The two offensives placed simultaneous demands on German fuel, rail transport and command attention, and their strategic purposes overlapped in the hope of disabling Soviet industry and communications. But operationally they were distinct: one threatened the central capital from the southwest, while the other pursued objectives in Ukraine and the Donets Basin. Keeping those axes separate prevents an atlas from turning all eastward arrows into one vague German advance.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–40, separate Army Group Centre and South tasks}}

<h2 id="a-city-at-work">A city preparing while armies moved</h2>

Around Moscow, civilians were constructing defensive works. The U.S. Army history notes that the workforce was largely women, building several semicircular belts, while workers' militia battalions prepared to man positions. This labor was part of the military story, not a decorative scene behind the tanks. A ditch had to be marked, dug and tied to a defended road; an obstacle had to be placed where it would block a vehicle rather than merely look formidable on a plan. Workers used time and physical strength in a city also trying to keep transport, factories and households functioning. Whether every new work would receive trained troops and ammunition in time was uncertain.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 37, women building Moscow defensive belts and workers' militia}}

The local defense had a social dimension as well. A militia battalion drawn from a factory might possess knowledge of its district and an urgent reason to defend it, but neither fact substituted for field training, antitank weapons or communications with army units ahead. Some civilians were evacuating or preparing to do so; others were assigned to remain at work. We should resist the temptation to describe every person in Moscow as sharing a single mood of resolve. The most dramatic panic and official evacuation orders belong to the following days. On 13 October the existence of fortification labor shows mobilization and fear together, not a reliable measure of the private thoughts of those holding shovels.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, emergency labor and militia in the capital}}

Transport linked the city's civil and military responsibilities. Roads used by incoming formations also carried wounded people and displaced civilians east. Rail cars assigned to factories or government offices could not at the same time bring forward every military reserve. Yet an evacuation could preserve skilled workers and equipment for later production. Historians of the Soviet war economy have shown how moving industry east kept essential output possible while straining receiving regions and causing interruptions. In October, commanders could not know which removal would succeed and which would leave a factory idle for months. The immediate decision was whether a machine should continue producing near a threatened front or be disassembled before German troops could reach it.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–40, the military threat and transport strain}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 3–6, military and economic stakes of the Moscow operation}}

The violence envisaged by German leadership gives this civil preparation its sharper context. The U.S. Army history records a 12 October instruction to surround Moscow and starve it rather than enter it immediately. A plan to reduce a city through hunger made civilians an intended object of military pressure. It also reveals why one should not narrate the campaign as a conventional race to plant a flag in the Kremlin. German commanders had differing tactical problems on the approach roads, but the political leadership's stated conception of the city included the suffering of its inhabitants. This fact does not explain every individual German movement on 13 October; it defines the consequence German policy sought if those movements succeeded.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 37, 12 October German directive on surrounding and starving Moscow}}

<h2 id="time-material-and-weather">The cost of each mile</h2>

German operational accounts often place mud at the center of the October slowdown. Mud mattered. Snow had appeared during the pocket fighting, and successive rain and thaw made roads increasingly unreliable. But 13 October cannot be explained by saying that weather suddenly stopped an otherwise unopposed army. Soviet soldiers continued to fight in the pockets and at new roadblocks. German units needed fuel, shells, spare parts and food; vehicles that had already travelled enormous distances required maintenance. Even a dry road could not carry unlimited traffic, and a bridge damaged by combat could force a queue. Weather raised the cost of every one of these tasks; it did not create them from nothing.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 39–40, weather, roads and stretched spearheads}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 3–5, German weakness and Soviet resistance alongside weather}}

The railway problem lay behind the forward road problem. Soviet tracks had to be repaired, often converted to a gauge suitable for German rolling stock, and linked to depots from which trucks or horse carts could carry supplies to fighting units. When the leading formation moved east faster than these supporting systems, its distance from the railhead grew. The immediate symptom might be an armoured commander asking for fuel, an artillery officer rationing ammunition or infantry waiting for bread. The military consequence was that a unit could possess tanks on the roster but be unable to concentrate them for a fresh attack. Statements about how many divisions Army Group Centre “had” on this date need to be measured against what those divisions could actually bring to a specific sector.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, transport and supply difficulties in the October advance}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 3–5, operational overextension before the Moscow battle}}

Horses were equally part of this army. German infantry and artillery relied on animal transport, which needed feed, care and passable routes. A motorized spearhead could overtake the men and guns intended to hold the ground it crossed. Conversely, slow roads might allow infantry to close part of the gap, though this did not automatically restore the vehicles or their fuel stocks. The relevant question is not whether the entire German army “stopped” on 13 October. It is which formation could move, which could fight after moving, and whether a new advance could be sustained along a broad front. Different answers applied around Kalinin, Mozhaisk and Mtsensk.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 39–40, diverging infantry and armour pace and use of horse carts}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 80–81, German transport constraints}}

The Soviet army had logistical constraints of its own. Rear areas were being compressed toward Moscow as wounded, refugees and retreating formations moved through the same infrastructure used by reserves. Incoming troops needed orders assigning them to specific sectors, and the capital's defensive works needed construction materials and tools. Fuel was not useful if it reached the wrong depot; a reserve without an accurate report of the threatened crossing might deploy late. What Soviet command gained from the Germans' delay was an opportunity, not a guarantee. The time mattered because people used it to assemble a defense under conditions in which a delay of even a day might alter where a battalion could be placed.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, emergency commitment of conscripts, veterans and civilian labor}}

This pressure also complicates the language of reinforcement. A new formation arriving near Moscow could strengthen the line only after its commander learned the ground, made contact with adjacent troops and identified where guns could cover the road. A trainload of soldiers was not a finished defensive position. A formation that had escaped an encirclement might know the enemy's methods but require weapons and a functioning headquarters. The armies defending Moscow were therefore being built and consumed at the same time. Each assignment narrowed one gap while another might open under German pressure. That is why counting arriving units, without asking what they could do at their assigned junction, gives a falsely reassuring impression of the Soviet position on 13 October.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, mixed Soviet formations and rushed deployment along Mozhaisk}}

<h2 id="claims-and-knowledge">What commanders could know</h2>

German public messaging ran ahead of the military situation. Otto Dietrich had told foreign correspondents on 9 October that the eastern campaign was decided. The German victories were large enough to give that claim an appearance of plausibility, yet his statement was propaganda made while Soviet forces still fought and while German supply and mobility problems were mounting. It is evidence of what officials wanted audiences to believe, not independent proof that the road to Moscow had become undefended. The confidence of a press briefing also affected expectations within Germany: a campaign said to be nearly won left little room in public discussion for a long winter struggle.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–39, Dietrich's 9 October announcement and the continued military crisis}}

News of the military crisis reached Moscow unevenly. On 11 October, the American ambassador Laurence Steinhardt reported that the Soviet public had received little warning until an 8 October communiqué acknowledged intense fighting around Vyazma and Bryansk. An admission concerning Orel followed. In his account, the abrupt appearance of these places in the news shocked residents accustomed to much vaguer reports of fighting along the front. This was the ambassador's observation of the information climate, not a survey of the city's population. It nonetheless helps explain why preparations for evacuation could provoke such alarm a few days later: the public had to absorb a sudden change in the apparent proximity and seriousness of the German threat.{{p4-frus864::Document 864, 11 October, opening paragraphs on communiqués of 8 October and the subsequent Orel announcement}}

One tempting retrospective story is that German headquarters missed a brief, open chance to enter Moscow immediately after Vyazma. The claim assumes that the troops needed for such a thrust were free, fuelled and able to hold what they reached. It also treats Soviet soldiers inside the pockets as already neutralized and the new defense as nonexistent. The contemporary task on 13 October was much less tidy: contain surviving Soviet forces, move on several roads, repair and supply the advance, then defeat whatever troops Zhukov could place in the approach sectors. A counterfactual rush might be worth discussing, but it cannot be converted into a lost certainty on the evidence of a line drawn between Vyazma and the capital.{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, forces retained for pocket containment}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–40, emerging Mozhaisk defense and slowing German advance}}

Nor should the Soviet response be turned into a fully formed triumph on this date. The defenses were fragile; Kalinin would fall on the fourteenth, and the perceived danger would help prompt extraordinary measures in Moscow. What the new command achieved by the thirteenth was a structure in progress. It could assign units to routes and bring civilian work into the defense, while no single stretch was secure simply because a front headquarters had named it. The difference between surviving the day and winning the campaign remained vast. It was precisely because the immediate situation was unresolved that central authorities had to consider the evacuation of offices and industry even while trying to prevent the city's capture.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, Kalinin's 14 October fall and subsequent panic}}

The road to Moscow on 13 October was therefore a struggle over the use of time. German spearheads had broken the earlier Soviet field defense but were divided among exploitation, containment and supply. Soviet command had lost armies but was gathering people into sectors that might make the next German movement costly. Workers were making physical barriers around a capital whose fate was still uncertain. None of these facts promised the outcome. Three days later, when the government evacuation became visible and panic spread, the military crisis would enter the city's streets. That change belongs to the next study; this one ends at the moment the defense was being made and its weakness was plain to those responsible for making it.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–40, the transition from encirclement to Mozhaisk defense and Moscow crisis}}
