<h2 id="the-day-before-another-attempt">The day before another attempt</h2>

On 14 November 1941 the German Army Group Centre was preparing to renew its attempt to defeat the Soviet armies guarding Moscow. The major mobile thrusts of the renewed attack lay just ahead, beginning on the following days; they were not accomplished facts on the fourteenth. A Soviet counterblow on the southern approaches near Serpukhov had, however, already begun that day. Around the capital, both armies remained capable of action and neither had solved its central problem. Fedor von Bock needed a decisive result from worn formations before deeper winter. Georgi Zhukov needed to hold a broad front while reserves were still forming and moving. The phrase “edge of winter” describes their deadline, not a climatic explanation for what would happen next.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48–50, 14 November Soviet Serpukhov counterblow and later German opening}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 83–86, last-minute German planning to 13 November and subsequent offensive}}

The phase began with German attacks from the south on 30 September and across the centre on 2 October. The Vyazma and Bryansk encirclements made the Soviet situation desperate, yet the capital did not fall. The chapters that followed traced a road whose meaning changed: from a route of German exploitation to a corridor requiring repair and supply, and then to a defensive system contested by Soviet forces. The Tikhvin study showed another German success that threatened a city's food. This closing chapter asks what all three army groups could actually do by mid-November. It does not repeat their October battles in full. It sets their present positions, resources and choices beside one another.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–42, October operation and simultaneous northern and southern campaigns}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–50, November disposition and Soviet defense}}

There was no settled German answer at the top. The meeting of army-group and army chiefs of staff at Orsha on 13 November considered how far the army might still advance and where it should stop. Army commander in chief Walther von Brauchitsch and chief of staff Franz Halder had to weigh exhausted units and extended railways against the possibility that Soviet resistance might yet fail. Halder's discussion separated conserving what Germany held from using its remaining strength for one more gain. By the evening he had retreated from the most expansive objectives but still wanted the army groups to press what they could before the year ended. This was a decision made under uncertainty, not the execution of a secure winter plan.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 42–45, Halder's conservation-versus-effect debate and 13 November Orsha conference}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 83–86, German strategic alternatives and Orsha meeting}}

<h2 id="the-three-army-groups">Three army groups, three problems</h2>

Army Group North still held Tikhvin, taken on 8 November, but possession of the rail junction had not delivered Leeb's full objective. Soviet forces were contesting the German salient, and the hoped-for link with Finnish forces near the Svir had not been made. The railway toward Ladoga remained cut while the town was German-held, so Leningrad's supply crisis persisted. But Leeb needed troops both to defend the salient and to maintain the broader siege line. The U.S. Army history reports that by this stage he had exhausted his reserves reaching Tikhvin. A commander can win a local objective and still be unable to turn it into the next strategic step.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 42–45, Leeb's reserve shortage after Tikhvin and unfulfilled further objective}}{{p4-cmh-leningrad::Chapter VII, printed p. 136, Tikhvin rail cut and continued lake supply}}{{p4-enstad-chronology::Cambridge chronology, 8 November capture}}

The Soviet response in that sector was developing rather than complete. Fifty-Second Army had begun an attack near Malaya Vishera on 12 November, while Fourth Army's larger attack on Tikhvin would not begin until later. Fifty-Fourth Army also had its own sector nearer the Volkhov. Those distinct dates matter on a 14 November map: one may show Soviet pressure already under way south of the salient, but drawing every later counteroffensive arrow as a present achievement would make a false reconstruction. The German position was vulnerable because its route was long and narrow; vulnerability was not the same thing as its having already been eliminated.{{p4-tikhvin-history::Official Russian campaign history, 52nd Army attack from 12 November and later 4th and 54th Army offensives}}{{p4-stavka-tikhvin::Archival catalogue, 9 November concentration order for 4th Army}}

Army Group Centre had the most politically charged objective. Bock's forces faced Moscow from a broad arc. Ninth Army held the north near Kalinin; Third Panzer Group stood west of Klin; Fourth Panzer Group and Fourth Army occupied the central approaches; Second Panzer Army faced Tula and the Oka from the south. Second Army covered the vulnerable far southern flank east of Orel and Kursk. This deployment was not a neat ring already closed around the capital. The north and south arms had separate routes and substantial Soviet forces between them and their projected meeting places. Tula remained Soviet-held, making it a live obstacle on the southern side.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48–49, second-week-November German dispositions and Soviet fronts}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 85–86, renewed envelopment plan and southern flank}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 39–41, Tula still Soviet-held}}

Army Group South under Gerd von Rundstedt was spread from the Donets region to Crimea. First Panzer Army was moving toward Rostov, while Seventeenth Army held along the Donets and Sixth Army had not resumed the advance its superiors wanted. In Crimea, Eleventh Army had made major gains, but Sevastopol remained a Soviet fortress; the Kerch Peninsula had not simply vanished from Soviet control. The German planning report at Orsha described Kerch as almost cleared, which is itself evidence that the process was not complete. A single southward arrow across the map would conceal the separate time and supply demands of these operations.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 85–86, Orsha report on First Panzer, Seventeenth, Sixth and Eleventh Armies}}{{p4-kirchubel-barbarossa::End Game, separate Rostov, Donbas and Crimea operations}}

<h2 id="what-orsha-revealed">What Orsha revealed</h2>

The 13 November Orsha meeting is unusually useful because it brings the German command's aspirations and its internal accounting into the same room. The U.S. Army history reports staff figures of roughly 686,000 German casualties by 1 November, one third of the eastern front's motor vehicles worn out or beyond repair, and panzer divisions with about thirty-five percent of their original tank strength. These are German staff estimates transmitted through a later history; they are not a count of men or vehicles standing at each location on 14 November. Their significance is that senior officers knew the combat system had been severely diminished before they asked it to move again.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–45, Orsha staff figures for casualties, vehicles and panzer strength}}

Those figures invite care. A tank temporarily under repair and one permanently lost did not have identical futures. A division's original tank strength was not necessarily the best denominator for judging today's infantry and artillery. A casualty total across the whole eastern front did not say whether Bock or Leeb had the most acute shortage in a particular battalion. Yet the trend was unmistakable in the staff discussion: trained people, mobile equipment and replacement capacity were limited. German planning could not honestly assume that an army-group order restored the June army at November's front. The same report noted that winter clothing was being left behind because rail capacity had to carry more urgent goods. Even an item manufactured and waiting in storage could be unavailable to a soldier if transport could not deliver it.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–45, staff estimates and winter-clothing transport constraint}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 83–85, infantry, armor, railway and clothing limitations}}

Halder's proposed “minimum” and “farthest” lines make another revealing aside to the map. Even his smaller line lay east of Moscow and would have required a connection toward the Svir in the north and Rostov in the south. The larger one reached toward Vologda, Gorky, Stalingrad and Maykop. These were planning constructs, not territories Germany controlled. They show that the leadership was debating how to close out a campaign whose original promise of a rapid decision had failed. At Orsha, staff officers from the field challenged the reach of those aims. The discussion narrowed, but Halder still pressed for more effect from the remaining weeks.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 42–45, minimum and maximum projected lines and 13 November revisions}}

German strategic time was becoming expensive. If the army stopped without defeating Soviet capacity, it would have to defend a long occupied frontier while Britain remained at war in the west. If it attacked, it might lose the men and machinery needed to survive winter and resume operations later. The German planning study records a suggestion by General Friedrich Fromm that a peace offer be considered; that was a suggestion, not an offer made or a policy adopted. Its appearance in the account is evidence that some officers recognized the danger of a continuing two-front struggle. Hitler's government had launched a war of conquest and destruction, however, and there is no basis to imagine a credible peace path merely because a staff officer mentioned one.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 83–85, Fromm suggestion and German strategic debate}}{{ushmm-eastern-front::USHMM, German war of annihilation and invasion aims}}

The Soviet side had its own costly arithmetic. Factories moved east could preserve future weapons production, but trains, workers and machines needed time to become productive again. Reserve armies could be assembled, but not all were equally trained or equipped. The Soviet defensive command therefore had reason to value every day Bock spent repairing roads, while also fearing that the delay would end before new units were ready. This asymmetry is better described as a competition in mobilization than as a morality play about weather. German losses reduced the power of an existing force; Soviet replacement required creating a usable new force. Both processes occurred on the same railway network and under threat of attack.{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed pp. 1 and 10, uneven Soviet industrial evacuation and restoration}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–49, Soviet reserve formations and varied readiness}}

<h2 id="moscows-defenders">Moscow's defenders</h2>

Zhukov's Western Front held the direct approaches to the capital while Ivan Konev's Kalinin Front guarded the northern flank and Soviet forces on the southwest guarded the southern connection. The U.S. Army history describes large reinforcements to Western Front in late October and the first half of November, but the precise arrival and usefulness of each formation varied. A newly arrived rifle division had to relieve troops in a sector, connect its communications and identify defensive positions. A brigade with tanks needed fuel and crews who knew the roads. Soviet command could increase the number of formations on paper faster than it could remove the friction of making them fight together.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–49, Western Front reinforcements and neighbouring Soviet fronts}}

Moscow itself had experienced evacuation, public alarm and emergency rule in October, yet it remained a functioning military and political centre. On 7 November Stalin reviewed a parade in Red Square and spoke to soldiers. His parade address invoked Russian military figures; in his speech the previous evening he had stressed the coalition with Britain and the United States. It would be wrong to let the image of the parade stand for an army already secure. A public ceremony could reassure residents and signal continuity while the front remained uncertain. It also sent troops toward combat. The ceremony belongs in the story because political authority and morale mattered to a capital under threat, but it could not substitute for guns on the western roads.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 41–42, 7 November parade, speeches and Allied coalition}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–50, continuing threat and Soviet preparations}}

The Anglo-American connection had also become more concrete since the October supply conference. In his 4 November reply to Roosevelt, Stalin accepted the proposed billion-dollar arrangement and its delayed, interest-free repayment terms. He also offered Soviet commodities needed by the United States and welcomed direct communication between the two leaders. The exchange reveals a relationship being built through reciprocal undertakings rather than a sudden erasure of political distrust. Its timing mattered: Moscow could present the commitment as evidence that continued resistance had powerful external support. Its battlefield effects still depended on ships, ports, railway wagons and allocation decisions. An agreement concluded in the Kremlin could strengthen the prospect of sustaining the war without immediately supplying a tank brigade west of Moscow.{{p4-frus803::Document 803, Stalin's letter of 4 November transmitted on 6 November, acceptance, commodities and direct contact}}{{p4-frus799::Document 799, proposed supply and financial terms of 30 October}}

On the ground, Tula remained the southern hinge. Soviet Fiftieth Army and local defenders held the industrial city despite German pressure. Guderian could seek a way around it, but the city could threaten movement and supply along the flank of a projected southern envelopment. In the north, German Third and Fourth Panzer Groups could plan routes toward Klin and the Moscow–Volga Canal, but Soviet positions and the distance between spearheads remained real obstacles. Zhukov's job was to keep these approaches connected as a defensive system, not merely to hold an imaginary circle at the capital's edge. The result on 14 November remained undecided.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48–50, Tula, German northern plan and Soviet defense}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 85–86, projected double envelopment and exposed Guderian flank}}

The Soviet counterblow near Serpukhov on 14 November captures that uncertainty. According to the U.S. Army history, Forty-Ninth Army, reinforced with cavalry, rifle and tank elements, attacked the right flank of German Fourth Army in the Serpukhov sector. Zhukov was reluctant about such “counterblows” because he expected the main German attack and doubted the value of spending forces in a limited blow. Stalin pressed for them. This was neither the great December counteroffensive nor a proof that Soviet initiative had decisively passed to every front. It was a contested command decision within an active defense.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 50, 14 November 49th Army counterblow and Zhukov-Stalin disagreement}}

The disagreement reveals something easily lost in a retrospective account of Soviet victory. Stavka had the authority to demand action on a threatened front, but the commander at that front had to judge whether the available cavalry, tanks and infantry could produce more than a brief disruption. A counterblow could force German commanders to protect a flank and reveal their dispositions. It could also consume a Soviet reserve precisely when another German thrust began elsewhere. The correct answer was not evident from Moscow's eventual survival. On 14 November Zhukov could see the danger of spending his formations while Stalin could see the danger of yielding initiative entirely. Both were making military decisions inside a political command structure whose decisions could be coercive and costly.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed p. 50, Zhukov's reluctance and Stalin's insistence on counterblows}}

The same tension operated at army-group scale on the German side. A division sent to support Leeb near Ladoga could not reinforce Bock near Moscow that week. A truck assigned to First Panzer Army's route toward Rostov could not haul winter clothing to an infantry regiment west of the capital. Headquarters could shift priorities, but those shifts took rail slots and time. Orsha's strategic language of conservation versus maximum effect was therefore expressed in concrete choices about vehicles, crews and routes. The German command's inability to meet all its objectives simultaneously was not a failure of will. It was a limit imposed by force size, geography and the continuing Soviet resistance each army group faced.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–45, Orsha resource and objective debate}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 83–86, transport limitations and simultaneous army-group operations}}

<h2 id="roads-weather-and-repair">Roads, weather and repair</h2>

The approach of freezing weather made the German decision more tempting. Hardening ground could let armoured units move beyond roads where autumn mud had confined them. It did not replenish tank strength or repair a worn engine. Short days limited flying time; low cloud could obstruct air support. A front prepared to exploit a temporary improvement in mobility still needed fuel at the right place and infantry able to follow. The army's supply chain had to convert or repair railway track, transport loads beyond the railhead and distribute them along a broad arc. Each additional mile east increased demand on vehicles already worn by months of war.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–51, frozen ground and limits on air and vehicle operations}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 83–85, rail, supply and equipment constraints}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 39–40, autumn mud and vehicle attrition}}

The staff debate about winter clothing is often compressed into the statement that Germany had none. The evidence is more precise and more revealing: material existed in rear storage but could not be brought forward in sufficient volume without displacing other urgent traffic. A frontline soldier's exposure therefore resulted from planning assumptions and a transport allocation, not from a mysterious inability to manufacture a coat. The Russian winter had been foreseeable as a season; what the German high command had not built was a supply system prepared to sustain this invasion through it. The consequence was felt unevenly by men whose unit, road and depot positions differed.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–45, Orsha staff report on clothing left in rear}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 83–85, delayed distribution of winter equipment}}

Soviet forces faced winter, too. Their ability to use local railways and interior reserves did not exempt a newly formed unit from cold, shortage or fatigue. A wounded soldier awaiting evacuation, a worker dismantling machinery and a driver pulling ammunition through mud all faced the season in different ways. Soviet success in building forces came with high human costs and imperfect equipment. To dispel the winter myth is not to deny physical suffering. It is to locate the causes: military resistance, distances, force attrition, supply choices, command expectations and changing ground conditions acting together.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–51, mixed Soviet reserve readiness and changed weather}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed pp. 1 and 10, costs of evacuation and industrial restoration}}

One must also separate repair capacity from combat count. A German panzer division with fewer ready tanks might restore some in workshops, but mechanics needed parts, fuel, shelter and time. Recovered vehicles could still break down along a road not suited to heavy traffic. A Soviet tank brigade sent to a threatened sector could be delayed for the same reason. Operational maps show formations as symbols because a symbol is legible; the symbol does not disclose whether its drivers have slept, whether its radios work or whether its trucks arrived. For Phase IV, the relevant question is not only where a unit was ordered to move but what it could actually bring to the position.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–45, panzer strength and vehicle serviceability}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–49, Soviet formation movement and readiness}}

<h2 id="civilians-and-occupation">Civilians and occupation</h2>

The combat zone's civilian experience varied across the enormous front. In Leningrad the German holding of Tikhvin threatened a supply route and intensified an already dangerous food shortage. Around Moscow, families lived under the possibility of a renewed attack while workers supported defenses and military production. Near Rostov and in Crimea, people faced moving fronts, evacuation and the risk of occupation. These lives cannot be collapsed into one generic story of hardship. Their common feature was that military decisions about roads, railways, cities and food reached into homes and workplaces. A transport junction could be a place where children would or would not receive bread weeks later.{{p4-cmh-leningrad::Chapter VII, printed p. 136, Tikhvin rail loss and Leningrad food}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–49, Moscow-area labour and defense}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 85–86, southern and Crimean military situation}}

German occupation also brought policies that cannot be explained away by the demands of a difficult campaign. Nazi Germany waged a war of annihilation against Soviet citizens, including mass shootings of Jews and the deliberate starvation of Soviet prisoners. These crimes existed behind the armies whose operational plans we have been following. They did not begin when the attack on Moscow failed; they were part of the invasion. A chapter focused on troop movement should mention this because the lines of control determined which populations were exposed to the occupying power. It should also avoid claiming a precisely dated local massacre for 14 November without a source that documents one.{{ushmm-eastern-front::USHMM, war of annihilation, civilian mass murder and starvation policy}}{{ushmm-pow-treatment::USHMM, German starvation, disease and shooting of Soviet prisoners in 1941}}

The German plan for Moscow itself made civilians a strategic object. The 12 October instruction discussed surrounding and starving the capital rather than accepting a straightforward urban occupation. By 14 November, that intended consequence remained conditional on a military result Germany had not achieved. It belongs in the analysis because the army group's proposed encirclement was not merely a geometric flourish. Closing roads and railways around Moscow would have affected food, evacuation and the lives of residents. Soviet defense of approaches and rail links was therefore about the capacity of a city to function as well as the survival of an army headquarters.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 37, German 12 October instruction concerning Moscow's isolation and starvation}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48–49, continuing Soviet control of the capital and rail approaches}}

People under German control in Ukraine and other occupied areas faced coercion and murder while the armies continued to debate their next objective. The southern advance in October had already taken Kharkov, but control of an industrial centre did not mean the invader had obtained its evacuated production. Civilians who remained were exposed to German rule; those who went east added to the transport and housing burdens of the Soviet interior. These outcomes followed from a campaign whose maps measured possession by colour. A responsible atlas must make room for both the military value of a city and the consequences for those who lived there.{{p4-judaica-kharkov::Encyclopaedia Judaica, capture of Kharkov and subsequent German persecution}}{{p4-harrison-industry::Public PDF printed p. 10, factory and worker evacuation}}

<h2 id="where-phase-four-ends">Where Phase IV ends</h2>

The German armies had not lost all initiative on 14 November. Bock was preparing a renewed operation; Kleist's First Panzer Army still approached Rostov; Leeb still held Tikhvin. Soviet defenders were likewise more than a static obstacle. The Western Front had received reinforcements, Forty-Ninth Army had begun a limited counterblow, and forces east of Leningrad were challenging the Tikhvin salient. Sevastopol and Tula remained under Soviet control. These dated facts matter more than an assertion that one side had already won or lost the campaign. The outcome visible from a later winter does not belong in this day's situation report.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48–50, Moscow disposition and 14 November counterblow}}{{p4-tikhvin-history::Official Russian campaign history, active 52nd Army attack}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 85–86, southern position and renewed German planning}}

The larger historical conclusion is that the invasion had outlived its original time frame. German leaders knew that the Soviet Union still possessed men and industrial potential, while their own forces had suffered losses that replacements could not immediately repair. That recognition did not end aggression. At Orsha the high command chose to press remaining strength toward objectives it hoped might improve its position before winter. The Soviet state chose to defend the capital, keep Leningrad supplied as far as possible and maintain resistance on other fronts. Neither decision was inevitable from the shape of the map. Each was made by institutions weighing incomplete information and accepting costs that soldiers and civilians would bear.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 43–45, Orsha assessment and decision to continue}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 47–50, Soviet reserve and defensive choices}}{{p4-cmh-leningrad::Chapter VII, printed p. 136, Leningrad supply pressure}}

The ground had begun to harden in some sectors, offering better movement even as colder weather imposed new burdens. German and Soviet forces were not equally equipped or positioned, and neither could ask weather to choose a strategy for it. The next military phase would test whether Germany could turn its still formidable combat power into a decision before the Soviet defense deepened further. That belongs to later dates. This phase closes at the moment before the decisive new thrust: a capital defended, a railway at Tikhvin cut, southern campaigns still active, and senior commanders on both sides uncertain how much strength remained in the opponent.{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 49–51, changing ground and impending offensive}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 42–45, unresolved army-group objectives at Orsha}}

The article's companion map should be read with that restraint. The supplied German situation-sheet sequence does not identify a sheet dated 14 November, and its 12 November sheet cannot be silently relabelled. Without verified local reconstruction, this atlas provides physical geography and earlier political context rather than fabricated front positions. The prose can responsibly identify broad army sectors, defended cities and the direction of planned attacks from dated military sources. It cannot place every company on a precise line at midnight. Accuracy here means telling the reader which facts are known, which are approximate and which belong to the days after this chapter ends.{{p4-loc-atlas::Library of Congress situation-sheet catalogue and dated-sheet limitations}}{{p4-cmh-moscow::Chapter III, printed pp. 48–50, broad army disposition and subsequent offensive sequence}}
