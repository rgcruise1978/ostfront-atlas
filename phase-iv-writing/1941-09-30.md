<h2 id="an-offensive-with-two-beginnings">An offensive with two beginnings</h2>

On 30 September 1941, the offensive usually remembered as the German drive on Moscow began on its southern flank. Heinz Guderian's mobile command attacked from the region south of Army Group Centre's main line, turning toward Orel while the larger blow farther north was still being prepared. The distinction matters. A map showing all three German panzer groups moving on this date would give the impression of a simultaneous assault that had not yet occurred. The main attack by the forces under Field Marshal Fedor von Bock began on 2 October. On 30 September the campaign entered a new phase unevenly, with one powerful thrust already under way and other formations assembling for their appointed hour.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, Directive 35 and the opening of TAIFUN}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–79, reassembly and the southern opening of the Moscow operation}}

That asymmetry is a useful way to enter the story. In the previous study, *Victory and Catastrophe*, the defeat of Soviet forces around Kiev and the murder of Jews at Babyn Yar shared a date without sharing a single explanation. The army's victory in Ukraine had made men and equipment available for another attack, but moving formations back into place took time; nor did victory erase the wear of the campaign. The victims of occupation were meanwhile exposed to German power regardless of whether the next military gamble succeeded. The killings at Babyn Yar continued on 30 September. We need not repeat their account here to recognize that the offensive did not create an interval in which occupation violence paused.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 33–35, Kiev and the return of mobile formations}}{{p3-ushmm-babyn::The two-day massacre of 29–30 September 1941 and its German perpetrators}}

The question for this chapter is therefore narrower and more revealing than whether Germany was “close” to Moscow. What could an army group actually bring to bear after three months of fighting, and what did its leaders expect a new encirclement to accomplish? The German high command was not simply ordering every unit to race for the capital's towers. It intended to break the Soviet field armies guarding the approaches, opening the route to Moscow and weakening the state's capacity to continue the war. German operational plans, Soviet defenses and the material means of moving both armies made that prospect uncertain even before the first vehicles ran into autumn rain.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, strategic purpose and initial dispositions}}

<h2 id="what-the-german-command-sought">What the German command sought</h2>

Hitler's Directive 35, issued on 6 September, restored the central army group to the role of principal striking force. The order followed a bitter argument over objectives. During August, German armour had been diverted from the direct approach to Moscow toward the Ukraine, while another transfer supported operations in the north. Those decisions contributed to the destruction of the Soviet Southwestern Front around Kiev. They also meant that the formations now expected to fight in the centre had to be recalled, repaired and supplied. A decision on a map was not the same as a division ready at its starting line.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 32–35, Directive 35 and transfers between army groups}}

The plan that emerged aimed first at the Soviet forces standing west of Moscow. German panzer groups were to penetrate on both sides of the defenders and meet beyond them; infantry armies would help widen the breaches and reduce the forces trapped inside. The northern and central mobile arms were directed toward the Vyazma area, while Guderian's southern formation moved toward the approaches to Orel and Bryansk. The precise commands and their movements will become the subject of the 2 October study. For 30 September, the essential point is that the German design required the synchronized destruction of several Soviet formations before an advance on Moscow could be treated as an accomplished fact.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, Army Group Centre and the planned encirclements}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 77–80, Moscow operation and the panzer groups' assigned roles}}

The German planning study shows how much movement lay behind the apparently simple arrows. Fourth Panzer Group's projected striking force near Roslavl included the 10th Panzer Division already in the area, two divisions assigned from army reserve, one from Army Group South and two from Army Group North. The northern concentration near Velizh under Third Panzer Group likewise drew on formations from the Leningrad sector. Guderian's group was to turn from its Ukrainian operations with five panzer divisions assigned to the wide southern sweep. These were proposed concentrations and roles, not proof that every listed division was present at full strength or had crossed its start line on 30 September. The transfers required lengthy lateral journeys and consumed transport that would soon be needed for the offensive itself.{{blau-planning::Printed p. 76, projected panzer-division concentrations near Roslavl and Velizh and Guderian's southern group}}

The operational language concealed a strategic wager. Germany had destroyed or captured enormous Soviet forces since June, yet the Soviet state had continued to raise armies, move industry and resist on separate fronts. German planners still hoped that another battle of annihilation would do what the earlier ones had failed to do: end organized resistance in the decisive theatre. The intended capture or isolation of Moscow mattered because the city was a governmental centre, a transport hub and a powerful symbol. But those were different kinds of value. Occupying a capital, cutting its rail connections, destroying an army west of it and forcing a government to capitulate were not interchangeable outcomes. The plan assumed that success in the first of these steps would help bring the others within reach.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–39, German expectations and the Moscow approaches}}

Army Group Centre's strength on paper has sometimes encouraged an easy description of overwhelming superiority. The U.S. Army history gives approximately 1.929 million men for the German army group and at least 1.25 million for the three Soviet fronts opposite it, but warns that the German total included a substantial auxiliary component. It judges the effective strength of the German combat divisions a much closer match. Stahel's published introduction likewise describes the exceptional concentration of divisions assembled for Typhoon while stressing the distance between operational victory and the hoped-for strategic end to the war. These figures are useful only when their categories remain visible. A man attached to a supply organization, a rifleman in a front-line battalion and a wounded soldier carried on a roll do not represent the same immediate combat power. Neither aggregate tells us how many tanks could move that morning, how much fuel reached them or where the opposing forces were concentrated.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, strength estimates and the distinction between total personnel and combat effectives}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 1–2, force concentration and strategic purpose}}

The German army group's large aggregate included auxiliaries; the Soviet number covered three fronts. Use them to frame the scale of the battle, not to derive a precise odds ratio for the fighting on 30 September.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, comparative strengths and the author's qualification}}</p></aside>

<h2 id="the-southern-arm-moves">The southern arm moves</h2>

Guderian's command had only recently been central to the Kiev encirclement. In August, his Second Panzer Group moved south from Army Group Centre's sector; in September its armoured spearheads met those of Ewald von Kleist's First Panzer Group east of Kiev. That success did not place Guderian's men back on the Moscow axis by magic. The distance travelled south, the fighting in Ukraine and the subsequent turn north all had to be absorbed by machines, crews and supply columns. When the southern wing attacked on 30 September, it began from a position shaped by the preceding campaign, not from a fresh reserve assembled behind an untouched front.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 33–35, Guderian's route through the Kiev operation and recall}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–77, return of Second Panzer Group and the 30 September attack}}

The immediate movement was toward Orel, a junction whose roads and railways connected the approaches to Bryansk and the country farther east. Guderian's thrust sought to cut into the southern side of the Soviet defensive system and prevent Bryansk Front from dealing with the northern arm as an intact force. That purpose is clearer than a story told only in kilometres of advance. An armoured column could pass a town or cross a line and still leave Soviet units fighting behind it. An operational breakthrough became strategically useful only if the army could support it, keep its roads open and exploit the disruption before Soviet commanders established a new line.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 77–81, southern axis and operational objectives}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, Bryansk Front and Guderian's southern role}}

This was not yet a settled German victory over Bryansk Front. Andrei Eremenko's front had been organized in the summer as German movements threatened the southern approach to Moscow. The Soviet command knew that Guderian's armour might become a danger there, although knowledge of a threat was not the same as the ability to counter it effectively. Soviet formations had suffered heavily, commands had been reorganized, and their positions had to cover a broad region rather than one road. The coming battle would expose these weaknesses. On 30 September, however, the historian must resist narrating the subsequent pockets backward into the opening attack. Eremenko still commanded fighting forces; no later German tally of prisoners describes their condition on the first day.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 33–36, creation of Bryansk Front and Soviet dispositions}}

Guderian entered the offensive as commander of Second Panzer Group; it became Second Panzer Army on 5 October. The new designation recognized a larger command responsibility without replenishing its vehicles or relieving its supply difficulties. It is useful to keep that change in view because the southern operation joined mobile penetration to tasks normally associated with an army: securing a wide flank, maintaining contact with neighbouring formations and controlling ground behind the advance. A tank force could win an encounter and still require infantry to make its success durable. The change of name did not abolish that division of labour.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 79–80, offensive sequence and 5 October redesignation}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 40, Second Panzer Army designation and strained transport}}

The demand for fuel shows how preparation for Typhoon extended beyond the assembly areas. German planners calculated that petroleum trains entering the eastern theatre would have to increase from twenty-two a day to twenty-seven between 17 September and 2 October, then to twenty-nine from 3 October. These were requirements, not a record that the railway delivered every scheduled train. They nevertheless expose the offensive's dependence on an enlarged flow from the rear before the main attack even began. Stocks had been depleted, and incoming fuel was often being issued immediately. Concentrating tanks around Moscow meant concentrating consumption as well as striking power, while the other army groups continued to demand their share. An interruption behind the front could therefore matter before a spearhead encountered a new Soviet position.{{blau-planning::Printed p. 72, petroleum shortages and planned daily fuel-train requirements}}

<h2 id="the-defenders-west-of-moscow">The defenders west of Moscow</h2>

The Soviet defense facing the central army group was not a single wall extending neatly across the map. Ivan Konev's Western Front held the principal western approaches; the Reserve Front under Semyon Budenny occupied positions farther back and in related sectors; Eremenko's Bryansk Front covered the southern danger. Their combined strength represented substantial Soviet mobilization after the summer disasters. It did not mean that every division was equally prepared, that all sectors had adequate antitank weapons or that the three front headquarters could respond to a fast-moving encirclement as a unified instrument. An army's existence in the order of battle and its power at the point of contact are separate questions.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, Soviet fronts, commanders and aggregate strength}}

The Soviet command had used the interval after the central fighting in August to rebuild its defenses. That interval was not a gift from German indecision alone: it was purchased in part by battles elsewhere, including Soviet resistance and losses in Ukraine and on the northern approaches. The armies west of Moscow benefited from time, yet they also carried the burden of earlier defeat. Replacements had to be trained and placed; headquarters needed communications; ammunition had to arrive. A newly fortified position could slow an attack, but if neighbouring forces were bypassed or command links broke, it might become an isolated strongpoint rather than a continuous front. This is why the exact sequence of German penetration and Soviet reaction matters more than an undifferentiated arrow toward the capital.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, Soviet rebuilding and opening situation}}

Nor should the Soviet side be reduced to an inevitable victim of German manoeuvre. Eremenko's front, Konev's front and the reserves had their own orders and choices, made under constraints of information and time. The Stavka had earlier sought to protect the Moscow direction while also demanding resistance on other sectors. From a central headquarters, a report of German armour moving toward one junction could be read alongside warnings from a different flank; the choice of where to commit the remaining mobile reserve could not wait for a historian's complete map. The danger of hindsight is especially acute for 30 September because the main German assault had not yet revealed its full pattern.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 33–36, Stavka decisions and the three defending fronts}}

Rebuilding the Soviet front was also a contest over railway capacity. Germany's advance had made the eastward movement of people and industry an urgent national task, while armies needed men and ammunition moving toward the battle. Mark Harrison's economic history emphasizes that a weapons factory depended on a wider system of power, materials, transport and skilled labour. Preserving a machine was only one part of preserving its output. In the territory threatened by the new offensive, decisions about evacuation thus carried a military cost in the present and a possible benefit in the future. The Soviet government was trying to keep its field armies supplied while preventing the next German advance from destroying the means of supplying their successors.{{p4-harrison-industry::Printed pp. 1 and 10, industrial interdependence and the disruption caused by evacuation}}

<h2 id="the-material-cost-of-a-new-beginning">The material cost of a new beginning</h2>

The German plan demanded concentration at the very moment the army was most dispersed. Panzer formations had gone north and south, then were called back into Army Group Centre's operational design. Men could be reassigned by order; their vehicles had to cover the roads. Damaged engines, worn tracks and missing spare parts accumulated through months of movement and fighting. The mobile formations still possessed formidable striking power, but their operational endurance depended on maintenance and on fuel arriving behind the spearheads. An attack's first day could be impressive while the condition of its transport already limited the days that followed. The U.S. Army's reconstruction emphasizes both the late-September reassembly and the gap between total personnel and effective combat power.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, late-September concentration and combat-effective qualification}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 71–77, equipment condition and reassembly before the Moscow offensive}}

Supply was also a geographic problem. Railways could move far more than trucks, but a train's usefulness depended on compatible track, working rolling stock, repair capacity and the distance from the railhead to a fighting unit. Once material left the train, a truck or horse cart had to carry it forward along roads shared with advancing troops, ambulances and rear-area traffic. A fast panzer group increased the distance its columns had to travel for every additional load of fuel or ammunition. Its apparent speed could therefore enlarge its own supply problem. These relationships were not unique to autumn, but they mattered more as the army tried to commit several mobile formations simultaneously rather than support a single local thrust.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 71–79, transport and equipment constraints before TAIFUN}}

The German command possessed troubling evidence well before the October mud. An army-wide return of 4 September put only 47 percent of its tank strength in the eastern theatre in the category available for employment. Another 23 percent awaited repair, while 30 percent was disabled. These were earlier theatre totals, not the readiness of Guderian's force on the morning of the attack. Their importance lay in the warning they gave the planners. Repairs were restoring some vehicles, but replacement engines remained scarce, and machines already worn through the summer could fail again after relatively short journeys. The planning history describes emergency repairs that might extend a vehicle's life by only another 200–250 miles. German headquarters was committing a formidable force whose durability was much less reassuring than its concentration.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 72–73, 4 September tank return, engine shortage and emergency repairs}}

Infantry and armour also moved at different rates. The panzer groups were designed to disrupt, turn and pursue; infantry formations had to secure routes, hold ground and reduce enemy positions left behind. An armoured vanguard could reach a junction without controlling the country between it and the rest of the army. That fact matters for the way this atlas should depict the first days of Typhoon. A point marking an advanced unit cannot be shaded as a wide zone of settled occupation. There were Soviet formations between and behind German columns; the coming encirclements were military processes, not coloured areas that appeared complete when a spearhead crossed a map line.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, design of the panzer penetrations and subsequent encirclements}}

Rail junctions were consequently more than prizes for the attacking spearheads. They were potential routes for the next load of ammunition, for replacement engines, for ambulances carrying the wounded back, and for Soviet forces trying to move reserves toward a threatened sector. Damaging or capturing a junction changed several possibilities at once, but only after traffic could actually use the route. A railway drawn across occupied territory did not guarantee that its bridges were standing or that trains with the right locomotives were ready. In this sense, the operational geography of the Moscow campaign consisted of corridors with capacity, direction and repair requirements, not simply lines joining cities. The following chapters will test advances against that material geography rather than treating every new place-name in a communiqué as a secure base.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 71–79, the transport basis of the new operation}}

<h2 id="moscow-asks-what-will-arrive">Moscow asks what will arrive</h2>

While commanders prepared and opened the offensive, Soviet officials were negotiating for the means to survive a longer war. The British and American missions led by Lord Beaverbrook and W. Averell Harriman had arrived in Moscow on 28 September. Their meetings with Stalin were taking place as the German southern thrust began. Harriman's telegram transmitted on 30 September is valuable because it does not describe a triumphant coalition supplying everything Moscow required. It records a Soviet leader pressing for tanks and mobile antiaircraft and antitank weapons, with discussions of requirements still incomplete. Stalin's estimate of German superiority in tanks appears in the document as his belief, not as an independently established ratio.{{p3-frus786::FRUS 1941, vol. I, document 786, Harriman telegram of 30 September, paragraphs 1–3 and 5–8}}

That distinction between a request, an agreement and an arrival is central to the military history. A promised tank did not defend a road outside Bryansk on 30 September. It had to be produced or released, shipped, unloaded, carried onward and assigned to a formation able to maintain and use it. Yet the requests were not peripheral diplomacy. They show that the Soviet leadership was considering the material balance of a continuing campaign at the same moment the German command hoped for a decisive autumn battle. Their negotiations also complicated German assumptions that another encirclement would leave the Soviet state without means or partners. The future volume of aid should not be projected backward into the opening of Typhoon, but the process of securing it had already begun.{{p3-frus786::FRUS 1941, vol. I, document 786, Stalin's requests and unfinished discussions}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 38–39, Moscow conference and the delay before supplies could be delivered}}

Harriman's report gives a revealing detail. Stalin said that even small light tanks would be useful if they carried a cannon and sufficient armour, and he preferred a large share of the requested tanks in that category. He also declined the initial British and American offer of field guns and mortars while emphasizing antiaircraft weapons able to accompany troops and antitank guns. The choices were not a complete statement of Soviet needs across the whole front. They were a negotiating position recorded by an American participant. Still, they show an urgent concern with the practical problem of stopping mobile attacks and protecting troops from air power, rather than an abstract appeal for “more equipment.”{{p3-frus786::FRUS 1941, vol. I, document 786, Stalin's tank specifications and requested antiaircraft and antitank weapons}}

It also says the requirements discussion was unfinished. The conversation belongs in the history of the offensive because it reveals Soviet priorities, but it must not be counted as equipment already available to troops outside Moscow.{{p3-frus786::FRUS 1941, vol. <h2 id="conquest-and-the-people-beneath-it">Conquest and the people beneath it</h2>

The southern thrust emerged from a campaign whose effect on civilians was already immense. The German advance through Ukraine had placed Kiev under occupation; the massacre at Babyn Yar continued through 30 September. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records the killing of 33,771 Jews on 29–30 September, a figure for the two days together, not a count to be assigned to either date alone. The armed forces' operations and the security authorities' murders were not separate worlds. Military conquest made occupation possible, while commands and institutions of the invading state exercised power over those trapped behind its lines. The sequence should not be flattened into an assertion that every combat unit took part in a specific killing. It is enough, and historically more exact, to identify the political and military conditions that enabled it and the documented perpetrators.{{p3-ushmm-babyn::USHMM, Mass Shootings at Babyn Yar, account of 29–30 September and attribution of the killing operation}}

Conquest had already placed civilians within reach of German institutions organized to persecute and kill. Babyn Yar makes the connection immediate: the massacre begun on 29 September continued while Guderian's offensive opened on the thirtieth. Its victims were not casualties of the new tank attack. They were Jews murdered in an occupied city by German perpetrators acting within the wider invasion. The distinction sharpens responsibility. Combat produced territorial access, but racial policy determined whom the occupiers targeted and what they did to them. A military success in Ukraine and a new offensive toward Moscow could coexist with deliberate mass murder behind the advancing armies. The campaign's continuation offered no protection to those already under occupation.{{p3-ushmm-babyn::Mass Shootings at Babyn Yar, 29–30 September massacre and perpetrators}}

Soviet prisoners faced a different but related danger. German authorities were already holding large numbers of captives from the summer battles in conditions of inadequate food, exposure and lethal violence. The prisoners' fate was not simply a matter of an army surprised by the quantity of men it had captured. Nazi policy treated Soviet soldiers as racially and ideologically inferior, and their custodians denied them protection and provision. This matters at the opening of an operation designed to produce another huge enclosure. The intended military result was the removal of Soviet armies from the field; for those captured, the consequence could be entry into a system that threatened their lives after their capacity to resist had ended.{{ushmm-pow-treatment::Sections on shelter, forced marches, starvation and shootings of Soviet prisoners during 1941}}

<h2 id="what-thirty-september-can-show">What 30 September can show</h2>

At the end of this first day, the new offensive existed as a real attack in the south and as an imminent wider assault in the centre. The German plan required coordinated penetrations against Western, Reserve and Bryansk Fronts; the plan had not yet achieved its intended encirclements. Guderian's advance threatened the southern Soviet defenses, but a movement arrow alone cannot tell us how much resistance lay ahead, whether transport would keep pace or how long formations left behind would continue to fight. The history of the first day is therefore a history of commitment and uncertainty, not of an outcome visible only to those who already know October's losses.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, chronology of the attack and Soviet fronts}}

The German leaders had reasons for confidence. Their forces had won vast encirclement battles, and the main operational concentration was formidable. They also had reasons for concern that no declaration of intent could remove: losses, worn equipment, a long transport system, Soviet armies still capable of mobilizing, and the need to secure a victory before their striking power deteriorated further. Soviet leaders faced a different mixture of capacity and danger. They possessed substantial forces west of Moscow but did not know the entire German attack as a completed pattern; they sought allied material help while preparing to fight with what was already at hand. Both sides made decisions under partial information. The German command's greater operational initiative did not give it knowledge of how the campaign would end.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–39, pre-offensive strengths and subsequent limitations}}{{p3-frus786::FRUS 1941, vol. I, document 786, Soviet material requests during the opening days}}

The next study begins on 2 October, when Army Group Centre's principal assault widened the battle. Only then did the operation's multiple arms move together against the Soviet fronts. That second opening was not a correction to the first: it was the culmination of a staggered plan. Preserving the two dates keeps us from imagining a single instant in which an entire army group surged forward, a Soviet defense collapsed and the road to Moscow stood open. On 30 September the war offered no such finished picture. It offered a southern advance, an uncompleted concentration, urgent Soviet decisions and a new contest over the time, people and material needed to continue fighting.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 34–36, 30 September southern attack and 2 October main opening}}
