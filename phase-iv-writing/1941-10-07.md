<h2 id="what-it-means-to-close-a-pocket">What it means to close a pocket</h2>

On 7 October 1941, the German offensive west of Moscow had become a battle of enclosures. The mobile groups that began their main assault on 2 October had driven around Soviet armies toward Vyazma; farther south, Guderian's formation threatened to fragment the Bryansk Front. To describe this as “the trap closing” is accurate only if we ask what closed, when and for whom. A meeting of forward German units could block a major road and make escape far harder. It did not cause every Soviet unit inside the new perimeter to surrender. It did not give the Germans secure control of all ground within the ring. The work of holding the perimeter, defeating attempts to break out and processing prisoners would continue well beyond the date on the map.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, rapid Vyazma encirclement and prolonged Bryansk fighting}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 78–80, closures near Vyazma and Bryansk and the time required to reduce the pockets}}

The preceding chapter described the German plan as a set of coordinated movements. Here the plan's success created a new operational dilemma. Mobile forces could keep driving east to exploit the rupture, but a large body of Soviet troops remained on their flanks and behind them. Leave too few forces to contain those troops and corridors might reopen; leave too many and the road toward Moscow would lose the very speed for which the panzer groups had been concentrated. This was not an abstract disagreement among generals. It concerned the placement of divisions, artillery and fuel on particular roads while tens or hundreds of thousands of people fought, fled or were captured within a changing battlefield.{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, panzer forces committed to holding the Vyazma perimeter and the dispute over exploitation}}

The two principal encirclement areas must be kept separate. The Vyazma operation turned around Soviet forces to the west of that city. The Bryansk fighting involved a more broken landscape of forests and dispersed Soviet formations farther south. Later German reports counted vast numbers of prisoners, but their totals differ between accounts and were compiled after prolonged fighting. A retrospective prisoner count cannot serve as the population of a perfectly sealed pocket on 7 October. The U.S. Army history explicitly notes that many of Eremenko's troops eventually escaped or remained in the woods and that German forces were tied down there into the third week of October. That is a reason to examine the two battles on their own terms rather than use “Vyazma–Bryansk” as one undifferentiated disaster.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, German prisoner claims, prolonged Bryansk fighting and escapes}}

<h2 id="vyazma-the-northern-enclosure">Vyazma: the northern enclosure</h2>

Vyazma stood on the road and rail system linking Smolensk to Moscow, with connections running north and south. Its position made it a logical meeting point for mobile formations moving around the Soviet Western and Reserve Fronts. Third Panzer Group under Hermann Hoth approached from the north, while Erich Hoepner's Fourth Panzer Group advanced from the south. Their aim was not simply to enter the city. If they could join to its east, Soviet armies defending farther west would lose the most direct routes of withdrawal. German infantry armies would then have to hold the outer and inner fronts of the enclosure while the mobile units sought a new task.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, Hoth and Hoepner around the Vyazma position}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 75–79, German plan for the north and south Vyazma pincers}}

By 7 October, German spearheads had achieved the crucial convergence around Vyazma. The achievement was grave for the Soviet formations west of the city, including armies from the Western and Reserve Fronts. It is still necessary to distinguish an operational ring from a continuous, secure fence. Roads, crossings and gaps mattered; troops inside could attack the perimeter, seek an opening or continue to fight in place. Troops outside could counterattack or try to establish a new defensive line. A map drawn at this scale often joins German points with a smooth line; the actual perimeter consisted of units assigned to sectors, each with its own casualties, fuel state, visibility and communications.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, encirclement west of Vyazma; numerical scope differs among accounts}}{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, forces needed to hold the eastern edge of the Vyazma pocket}}

Fourth Panzer Group's task illustrates the cost of closing the ring. Kluge required Hoepner to keep a strong eastern perimeter; Georg Stumme's XXXX Panzer Corps and Heinrich von Vietinghoff's XXXXVI Panzer Corps consequently spent much of their effort containing Soviet troops. On 7 October, Hoepner sent Adolf-Friedrich Kuntzen's LVII Panzer Corps toward Kaluga and the SS division *Das Reich* toward Gzhatsk. These were limited releases, not the whole mobile group rushing east. Hoepner resented the restraint, but the Soviet pressure on the perimeter gave Kluge a military reason for it. The disagreement concerned how much risk to accept behind the advance, and which formation would bear that risk.{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, named corps holding the eastern perimeter and formations released on 7 October}}

Around Vyazma, mobile formations needed for pursuit were also needed to hold the perimeter. The operational success therefore consumed some of the very force intended to exploit it.{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, eastern Vyazma perimeter and the containment-exploitation dilemma}}</p></aside>

<h2 id="bryansk-a-less-neat-battle">Bryansk: a less neat battle</h2>

The southern German movement had begun earlier and reached a different kind of battlefield. Guderian's Second Panzer Group advanced through the Orel direction while Second Army helped form the wider operation against Eremenko's Bryansk Front. The German design sought to cut the Soviet front into groups that could be contained and destroyed. Forests, distance and Soviet movement made the resulting enclosures less tidy than the classic image of a single pair of pincers meeting at one point. German units could capture a road or town while Soviet formations still moved through nearby woods, fought behind them or sought to regain contact with their command.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 35–36, the Bryansk operation and continued Soviet movement}}{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 78–80, southern envelopment and the Bryansk fighting}}

The southern battle was especially difficult to summarize because its enclosures lay on different sides of Bryansk. The German account describes Soviet formations pressed into pockets southwest and northeast of the city, with Second Army and Guderian's mobile forces sharing the work of containment. Capturing a junction could disrupt Eremenko's communications without ending the fighting throughout either enclosure. Forest tracks offered routes that did not appear as major roads on an operational map, and German forces could not occupy every interval immediately. Some Soviet troops subsequently escaped east; others remained behind the German front. The persistence of these groups made the Bryansk operation a continuing demand on German troops, rather than a completed preliminary to the march on Moscow.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, separate Bryansk pockets, continued fighting and Soviet escapes}}

The terrain study describes the southern advance between river systems including the Desna and its tributaries, through wooded and marshy country. Its authors emphasize the urgency of reaching firmer routes and a railway near Orel before the wet season. That logistical concern helps explain the two-day early start of Guderian's attack and the importance German commanders attached to movement before road conditions worsened. Yet the document is also a postwar German interpretation with a familiar tendency to assign difficulties to nature. Its topographical observations do not cancel the independent fact of Soviet resistance or prove that every delay after 7 October had the same environmental cause. The river basins formed a battlefield, not an alternative actor that fought in place of the Red Army.{{p4-cmh-terrain::Printed pp. 10–11, Desna-area corridors, Orel rail route and early-start rationale}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36 and 39–40, Soviet resistance and later road deterioration}}

That distinction corrects a common way of narrating the week: the claim that one German closure simultaneously destroyed both the Vyazma defenders and the entire Bryansk Front. Even the German-centred U.S. Army account describes Bryansk as a protracted operation and notes later escapes. Eremenko's troops were not thereby spared a severe defeat. Large formations were disrupted, roads were cut and the coherence of the front was badly damaged. Yet “defeat” names a military result that unfolded through several days; it does not mean that every person within the German operational outline became a prisoner when a commander drew a circle on 7 October.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, difference between Vyazma closure and Bryansk pockets, including later escapes}}

The German army also had to decide how much force to leave behind in the forests. Every battalion guarding a supply route or reducing an isolated Soviet position was a battalion unavailable for immediate pursuit. Every surviving Soviet group capable of striking a road threatened the transport of fuel, ammunition and food to the leading troops. This is how resistance could matter even when it could not reverse an encirclement. The effect was measured in the time and forces required to make the German advance safe, not only in the number of towns a surrounded formation held. The struggle at Bryansk therefore affected the pace of Typhoon long after German reports could announce a large victory.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, Second Army and Second Panzer Group tied down into the third week of October}}

<h2 id="soviet-choices-inside-the-rings">Soviet choices inside the rings</h2>

For Soviet commanders caught west of the German spearheads, the problem was immediate and practical. Could they maintain communications with neighbouring formations? Was a road east still open? Was there fuel for the vehicles carrying guns, wounded personnel and headquarters records? Could an army fight toward a gap before German infantry and artillery strengthened it? Those questions varied from sector to sector, and this chapter does not invent a single order that all encircled units followed. What the surviving operational histories establish is that Soviet forces continued to resist, that some escaped the Bryansk fighting and that the German army had to devote substantial effort to containment.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, continued fighting and escape from the Bryansk area}}

The loss of contact between a front headquarters and its subordinate armies did not erase those armies as fighting organizations. Units could act on old orders, follow local commanders, try to move toward the sound of battle or seek routes through woods and villages. Conversely, a headquarters might issue a new instruction without knowing whether the intended recipient could receive it. This is one reason German prisoner counts cannot be translated into a contemporaneous map of Soviet control. The human and organizational process of fragmentation was not instantaneous. Soldiers made decisions amid imperfect information; some were captured, some died, and others rejoined Soviet lines or survived behind the advancing Germans.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, Bryansk Front's prolonged resistance and later escapes}}

For an encircled soldier, surrender and escape were not equal choices in a neutral military exercise. The Germans' treatment of Soviet captives made capture dangerous, although a soldier inside the pocket could not know the later mortality figures now available to historians. A wounded man unable to march, a gun crew seeking ammunition and a staff officer trying to locate an eastern route faced different possibilities even within the same army. The operational source records the direction and persistence of resistance; the museum's research records what often happened to those taken prisoner. Reading them together prevents a familiar narrative shortcut in which thousands of people vanish from view when their unit symbol disappears from a map. It also guards against the opposite romantic shortcut, imagining that every survivor became a partisan or that every break-out attempt succeeded.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, continued resistance and mixed outcomes around Bryansk}}{{ushmm-pow-treatment::USHMM, Treatment of Soviet POWs, custody and mortality during 1941}}

Soviet leadership in Moscow faced a separate task: to keep a defensible line between the pockets and the capital. The German advance threatened to open a wide gap, but using reserves effectively required knowing where the spearheads would emerge and which defending forces could still be recovered. Georgy Zhukov's later assumption of command over the combined Western and Reserve Fronts belongs to the next phase of this crisis. On 7 October it would be an error to credit him with orders he had not yet given in that role. The Soviet defense was being reorganized under pressure, and that process, rather than a suddenly completed new line, will be the subject of the 13 October study.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, Zhukov's 10 October command appointment and Mozhaisk assignment}}

<h2 id="numbers-and-the-people-counted">Numbers and the people counted</h2>

The defeat was immense, but a precise prisoner total is less secure than the familiar rounded figures suggest. The German planning history reports 658,000 captives from the two operations combined. The later U.S. Army synthesis distributes its totals differently, assigning 663,000 to Vyazma and about another 100,000 to Bryansk. Those accounts should not be added together or presented as mutually corroborating figures. They concern retrospective German counts, with boundaries and compilation dates that this evidence does not reconcile. The safe conclusion is a loss of hundreds of thousands of Soviet soldiers through capture across the October encirclements. None of those eventual totals describes a completed surrender on 7 October, when substantial forces remained in action.{{blau-planning::Printed pp. 79–80, combined German prisoner claim}}{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, different distribution of retrospective prisoner totals and continuing fighting}}

The people behind those totals entered a system in which capture could be deadly. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents the systematic neglect, starvation, exposure and shootings of Soviet prisoners of war in German custody during 1941. Its account describes inadequate shelter and food, brutal marches to the rear, and very high mortality during October. These conditions were not simply the unavoidable consequence of transport difficulties, nor can a later death rate be assigned to a specific captive on 7 October. German policy and the way authorities chose to handle prisoners mattered. An operational history that ends a soldier's story at the word “captured” omits a major consequence of the encirclement battles.{{ushmm-pow-treatment::USHMM, Treatment of Soviet POWs, sections on shelter, forced marches, starvation and October 1941 mortality}}

For the attacking army, prisoners were also a logistical burden, though that phrase must not soften the crime of their treatment. Guards had to be assigned, columns directed and routes used while the same army tried to move fuel and ammunition east. A government could make provision for feeding and sheltering those in its custody; Nazi Germany's treatment of Soviet prisoners shows the lethal consequences of its policies and priorities. We should not use the army's supply strain as an explanation that absolves those choices. The army's own operational needs and the prisoners' survival were competing claims on resources under a regime that devalued Soviet lives.{{ushmm-pow-treatment::USHMM, Treatment of Soviet POWs, custody, marches and camp conditions}}

The figures also obscure those who were not counted. Some soldiers escaped the Bryansk area; others hid in forests and later emerged in Soviet territory or in partisan formations. Some died before anyone could register them. Civilians displaced by the battle would not appear in a prisoner tally at all. The losses of Western and Reserve Front armies and the disruption of Bryansk Front had strategic weight even when exact personal outcomes remain uncertain. The most honest description is therefore qualitative as well as quantitative: a severe Soviet defeat, a German operational success, a continuing struggle within the pockets, and an immense human danger for those brought under German custody.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, Soviet escapes, woods and continued Bryansk fighting}}{{ushmm-pow-treatment::USHMM, fate of Soviet captives during the 1941 campaign}}

This distinction between an official count and a lived outcome also changes how we judge speed. A report announcing a high number of prisoners might persuade a distant headquarters that the military problem was largely solved, while officers on a particular road still faced a Soviet group trying to break through. Conversely, a Soviet front headquarters might still list a subordinate formation that had lost contact or had been split into several bodies. Each organization had incentives and delays in reporting. A later total can be indispensable for estimating the scale of loss, but it cannot tell a commander what was known at the exact hour he had to assign a division to guarding or pursuit. The historian's chronology needs those two levels of knowledge kept apart.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed p. 36, German retrospective totals alongside continued Bryansk operations}}{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, disagreement over forces kept at the Vyazma perimeter}}

<h2 id="another-order-in-the-rear">Another order in the rear</h2>

The same date also offers evidence about what German military rule meant away from the panzer spearheads. The Holocaust Museum's archival case study of the 1st Battalion, 691st Infantry Regiment, in Army Group Centre's rear area records that its commander, Major Alfred Commichau, ordered company commanders **on or about** 7 October to kill Jewish civilians in their respective areas. The date is approximate in the source, and the villages were in occupied Belarus, not at Vyazma or Bryansk. Those qualifications matter. This was not an incident inside either pocket, but it occurred within the army group's wider occupied zone while the command celebrated its offensive.{{p4-ushmm-ordinary::USHMM, Ordinary Soldiers, printed pp. 8–10, approximate date, battalion, order and rear-area location}}

The case is unusually well documented because a postwar investigation and trial produced evidence about how the order was handled. One company commander reportedly refused; another complied; a third initially hesitated, then passed the killing task to a subordinate after receiving a written order. The record does not license an easy moral fable in which refusal was normal or murder was an automatic response to discipline. It shows differences in individual decisions within an institution that made the order possible. The Jewish civilians targeted were people under occupation, not combatants. The language of “antipartisan” warfare used in the rear area was employed to associate Jews with an alleged threat regardless of individual conduct. The case study explicitly warns how German authorities conflated security operations with anti-Jewish violence.{{p4-ushmm-ordinary::USHMM, Ordinary Soldiers, printed pp. 9–16, company responses and antipartisan pretext}}

Why include this in a chapter on the encirclements? Because the war's geography had a rear as well as a front, and the authority used to control roads and villages behind Army Group Centre could be used to murder their residents. German commanders worried about bypassed Soviet soldiers and supply routes. That operational concern did not make Jewish civilians into partisans. In the Belarusian case, the museum's study finds that documented organized partisan activity in the area was limited at this stage, even as German commanders constructed a sweeping threat. The link between military control and civilian persecution is not that every action at the front caused a corresponding killing. It is that conquest furnished territory, armed men and command structures for policies directed at people who posed no battlefield threat.{{p4-ushmm-ordinary::USHMM, Ordinary Soldiers, PDF pp. 9–13, rear-area command, limited documented partisan activity and murder order}}

The source permits a close account of the decision chain, but not a fictional reconstruction of the victims' last conversations. It identifies the unit, the approximate date, the character of the order and contrasting company responses. The Jewish families in the affected villages appear most often through the paperwork and testimony of their persecutors. That imbalance is a property of the surviving record, not a reason to describe them as an anonymous background. The chapter can name the violence and the people targeted while acknowledging what the documents do not preserve. It can also keep separate the experiences of Soviet soldiers trying to escape the pockets and those of Jewish civilians attacked by a rear-area battalion. Both were exposed to German power in October; their situations and the evidence for them were different.{{p4-ushmm-ordinary::USHMM, Ordinary Soldiers, printed pp. 8–17, evidentiary basis and differentiated company actions}}

<h2 id="weather-time-and-the-road-ahead">Weather, time and the road ahead</h2>

The first snow fell on the night of 6 October, according to the U.S. Army narrative, and rain and snow soon began to deteriorate the roads. On 7 October, then, weather entered a campaign already complicated by the need to contain pockets and supply advancing formations. It is too simple to say that mud abruptly saved Moscow on the day the ring closed. German troops had won a major operational battle; Soviet troops were still fighting; and the German army needed to turn that victory into a further advance before its mobility ebbed. Weather changed the cost of movement, but it did not create all the other claims on trucks, fuel and infantry.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 39–40, first snow and subsequent road deterioration}}{{p4-stahel-typhoon::Introduction excerpt, printed pp. 3–4, weather as one impediment alongside Soviet resistance and German weakness}}

The geography of the pockets multiplied those claims. A panzer division holding a perimeter was stationary for a reason: Soviet forces inside might break through or threaten a route. Its tanks could not simultaneously stand guard and be the spearhead many miles farther east. Infantry needed time to arrive and take over. Prisoner columns moved back along roads that supply trucks wanted to use in the opposite direction. The physical problem of passing traffic through a few corridors was sharpened by the command problem of deciding when a pocket was sufficiently contained to release mobile formations. Stahel's description of Hoepner's limited 7 October onward movements illustrates the trade-off without asking us to endorse the commander's later complaint that a different decision would have guaranteed Moscow.{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, Hoepner's limited 7 October thrusts and containment disagreement}}

Even the phrase “move east” hides a choice of route and objective. A formation committed toward Kaluga was not following precisely the same corridor as one sent toward Gzhatsk, and neither could assume that a road beyond the next junction was free of Soviet opposition. The units assigned to protect an inner perimeter had to coordinate with those advancing outside it, or a Soviet breakout might cut a supply path behind the attackers. This is why the difference between a mobile unit's farthest point and the army group's operational reach matters. The farthest point could advance quickly; sustaining a broad front around a pocket and into new territory required a continuing flow of men and material that no dramatic arrow could guarantee.{{p4-stahel-exploiting::Public chapter 6 summary, 7 October directions toward Kaluga and Gzhatsk and forces tied to containment}}

For the Soviet command, every day of fighting in and around the enclosures was also a day in which another defensive position might be manned, a reserve might arrive or a threatened army might withdraw from a different sector. That does not turn the destruction of the Western and Reserve Front formations into a Soviet success. It identifies the military value of delay amid defeat. A road blocked to a German column for hours mattered only in relation to what Soviet commanders could do with the hours. By 13 October, the next date in this atlas, the Mozhaisk sectors and the reorganization of the defense west of Moscow would become the central question. The seeds of that response lay in the time created by continued resistance, even as the pockets brought terrible losses.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, prolonged pocket fighting and subsequent Mozhaisk defense}}

This date therefore holds two conclusions together. German arms achieved a profound operational success by isolating major Soviet formations, but the process of converting that success into a secure and exploitable situation remained incomplete. The people caught in that process included soldiers still fighting inside the enclosures, prisoners entering a lethal system and civilians in occupied rear areas subject to German military power. The map should not hide them behind a completed circle. The next chapter follows the attempts to build a new front from the damaged Soviet forces and to exploit the breach with an attacking army already forced to divide its strength between pursuit and containment.{{cmh-blitz::Chapter II, printed pp. 36–37, encirclement and defense sequence}}{{p4-ushmm-ordinary::USHMM, Ordinary Soldiers, printed pp. 8–17, rear-area violence on or about 7 October}}
